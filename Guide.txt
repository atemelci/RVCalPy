## 1. Which method should I use?

| Your target | Method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Single star (SB1), cool, line-rich | `ccf` | Fast, robust, one peak to fit |
| Single star, **very hot (OBA)** | `ccf` (default mask mode) | The hydrogen lines are excluded from the mask, so the Balmer wings cannot dominate the correlation |
| Binary (SB2), triple (SB3) | `bf` | The method is linear, so each component appears as its own sharp peak and is measured independently |
| Contact / near-contact binary with blended peaks | `bf --profile rot` | Rotational profiles fit nearly-touching components without the Gaussian's blending bias |
| SB2 whose peaks have merged into one hump | `todcor` | The blend is *modelled* (two shifted templates at once) instead of fitted around. Use it when the separation drops below about one profile FWHM — see [section 5](#todcor--two-dimensional-correlation-blended-sb2s) |
| A whole time series → RV curve | `batch` | Runs either method over all files and folds the result on an ephemeris |

**CCF (Cross-Correlation Function)** — a **weighted line-mask CCF**
(Baranne et al. 1996; Pepe et al. 2002; the construction applied by
Pino et al. 2018, A&A 619, A3): the mask holds the line cores of the
template weighted by their depths, and the CCF is the weight-averaged
observed line depth over the trial-velocity grid. The continuum and the
pressure-broadened hydrogen wings drop out of the correlation, and the
hydrogen Balmer/Paschen series is excluded from the mask by default
(`--keep-balmer` restores it) — this is what makes the CCF reliable for
**very hot (OBA) stars**, where a full-spectrum correlation is dominated
by the Balmer wings and biased by emission-filled H cores. The peak is
measured with a Gaussian on a linear baseline over a ±2 FWHM window
(the contrast fit of Pino et al. 2018). A full-spectrum
cross-covariance is available as `--ccf-mode template`. Unless `--rv-min` /
`--rv-max` are given, the RV search is **two-stage** (Katz et al. 2025,
A&A 704, A294, SOPHIE practice). A coarse ±900 km/s scan in 10 km/s
steps locates the peak, so any star within ±900 km/s falls inside the
window, and the fine `--rv-step` grid is then laid around it. No
starting guess is needed. The search is benchmarked on 16 UVES spectra
of **high-velocity metal-poor stars from −409 to +510 km/s**, compared
with the published velocities of the same spectra (Alazzawi et al.
2026). Over the 919 km/s span the slope is 1.000171 ± 0.000479 and the
median |difference| is 0.486 km/s ([benchmark report](README.md#benchmark)).
The BF window (`--vel-range`, default ±500 km/s) and the TODCOR window
(`--max-rv`, default ±200 km/s) are fixed rather than searched. Widen
them for a star beyond those limits.

**The CCF cleans the spectrum first**, in every mode: spikes are rejected
and the continuum is re-flattened — the step `todcor` uses, with one
guard: a pixel between 0 and 1.2 is never a spike, so the cores of deep
lines stay. **The RV error is the larger of two errors**: the
maximum-likelihood error of Zucker (2003) from the width of the
correlation peak, and the chunk error from the scatter of the RV between
five wavelength chunks of the spectrum. See [Cleaning and the RV
error](#cleaning-and-the-rv-error).

**BF (Broadening Function, Rucinski 1992/2002)** — the observed spectrum is
modelled as the convolution of the template with a velocity-space profile,
solved linearly via Singular Value Decomposition. Because the method is
linear, the components of a binary (SB2) or triple (SB3) appear as separate
sharp peaks, so each component's RV is measured independently. Recommended
for binary and multiple systems. The spectra enter the SVD as line depths
(1 − flux), following Rucinski: the continuum then drops out of the linear
problem, the BF baseline sits at zero and the wings decay cleanly. The
velocity step defaults to a quarter of the instrumental FWHM when the
resolution is known, and to the reference stepV = 5 km/s otherwise
(`--dv` overrides — see
[section 9](#9-bf-velocity-step-and-the-reliability-of-the-rv-errors)).
The RVs and their errors come from a Bayesian fit of the component
profiles to the spectrum itself ([section 9](#bayesian-velocities-and-errors)).

Several practices are adopted from the
[saphires](https://github.com/tofflemire/saphires) package:

- multi-order (echelle) spectra get **one BF per order**, combined with
  1/σ² weights from each order's BF sidebands, so noisy orders are
  down-weighted automatically;
- the BF is smoothed by the **instrumental FWHM c/R** when a
  resolution/spectrograph is given (R does **not** degrade the template in
  the BF flow — that would count the instrumental profile twice; use
  `--degrade-template` only for ultra-sharp synthetic templates);
- multi-component fits are sums of Gaussians on a **shared constant
  baseline**, and the noisy BF edges are trimmed before fitting;
- unphysical pixels (normalized flux > 1.2 or < 0) are interpolated over
  before the SVD;
- the barycentric correction is applied with the relativistic cross term,
  `RV = RV_measured + v_bary + RV_measured·v_bary/c` (Wright & Eastman 2014),
  from the observatory's geodetic coordinates (so the diurnal Earth-rotation
  term is included), the way the MIDAS `comp/bary` command uses the site
  longitude and latitude.

## 2. Quick start

Install first (see [Setup](README.md#setup)), then run the bundled data in
`examples/` — one **real** spectrum per method, and a **single** synthetic
template that serves all three:

| File | What it is | Method |
|---|---|---|
| `example_CCF_SOPHIE_norm.txt` | TYC 3844-385-1 — SOPHIE, OHP 1.93 m, 387–694 nm | `ccf` |
| `example_BF_HARPS_norm.txt` | LL Aqr — HARPS, ESO 3.6 m (archive programme 084.D-0591(C)), 378–691 nm | `bf` |
| `example_TODCOR_NeoNarval_norm.txt` | HD 105637 — Neo-Narval, TBL 2 m (Pic du Midi), 568–919 nm | `todcor` |
| `template_5215K_Logg3.txt` | synth3 synthetic spectrum, 5215 K, log g 3.55, 4500–6500 Å | all three |

The three spectra are observed data, in the continuum-normalized two-column
ASCII that `normalize` writes, each with the observation's metadata in its
`#` comment header — `rv_analysis.py header <file>` prints it. The
coordinates, the epoch, the site and the barycentric frame are therefore
read off the file, and none of them has to be typed on the command line.
The template is the raw synth3 output, line list and all: the reader skips
the header block and takes the 367 725 wavelength points below it — 0.006 Å
at 6000 Å, i.e. 0.3 km/s per point, well inside the 1 km/s TODCOR grid.

**One template for three different stars is deliberate.** The velocity is far
less sensitive to the template's temperature than to the data: LL Aqr is
≈ 900 K hotter than this template, yet its two velocities come out within
0.1 km/s of the matched 6093 K template used in the
[benchmark run](README.md#benchmark), and within 0.25 km/s of the published
values. One G-type template is therefore enough to demonstrate all three
methods on three different stars. Match the spectral type for production
work — a mismatch shows up in the line-check figure, in the peak shape and,
first of all, in TODCOR's fitted α — but do not read "one template per star"
into this set.

The template also does not have to span the spectrum. It covers
4500–6500 Å against spectra reaching from 378 to 919 nm, and each command
restricts itself to the overlap on its own (5686–6495 Å for the Neo-Narval
file, which starts at 568 nm).

### `ccf` — a single clean peak

```bash
python rv_analysis.py ccf --spectrum examples/example_CCF_SOPHIE_norm.txt \
    --template examples/template_5215K_Logg3.txt \
    --spectrograph SOPHIE --no-bary
```

```
Star                : TYC 3844-385-1
CCF method          : weighted line mask, 3163 lines (Baranne 1996; Pepe 2002; Pino et al. 2018), hydrogen series excluded, spectrum cleaned (spikes, continuum)
Continuum S/N       : ~405 per pixel
BJD = 2461123.405687
RV = -35.7965 ± 0.0514 km/s  (no barycentric correction applied)
RV error            : from the CCF width (Zucker 2003): C = 0.8559, N = 199789 px; profile-fit error 0.0067 km/s; chunks: 0.0514 km/s (K = 5 of 5, q = 1.15, tau = 0.0914 km/s); adopted 0.0514 km/s = max(formal, chunks)
v_bary = -7.8077 km/s (for information only, not applied)
```

The error is the larger of the width error (0.0090 km/s here) and the
chunk error (0.0514 km/s). At this S/N the chunks set it: their velocities
scatter by tau = 0.09 km/s more than their noise allows, which the width
error cannot see (see [Cleaning and the RV
error](#cleaning-and-the-rv-error)).

Every run names the **star** at the top of its report (`Star : ...`). The
result tables carry no star column — they hold the measured values only. The
name is `--object` when you give one (the GUI's **Target** box), and otherwise
the `OBJECT` the spectrum's own header carries, or, for a convention that puts
the target in the file name, the name read from there. A spectrum that names
no target anywhere still runs: the star name is a label, not an input to the
measurement.

`--no-bary` is **what this file needs**: the SOPHIE header says the
wavelengths are already barycentric, so applying the correction again would
double it. If you leave it out, the run is not stopped: it warns and applies
v_bary, because the decision is yours ([section
7](#7-barycentric-frame-what-the-headers-say-and-who-decides)). v_bary is still
computed and written to the result file as a note, and cross-checked
against the pipeline's own BERV (−7.8077 vs −7.8121, +4 m/s: OK).

Running `bf --components 2` on the same file returns nearly the same
velocity, −35.7911 ± 0.0178 km/s, and a second "component" that is a broad
flat pedestal (amp 0.0006, σ = 43 km/s, RV error 18 km/s) rather than a
star. The epoch is
single-peaked, which is exactly when `ccf` is the right command.

### `bf` — the two components of an SB2

```bash
python rv_analysis.py bf --spectrum examples/example_BF_HARPS_norm.txt \
    --template examples/template_5215K_Logg3.txt \
    --spectrograph HARPS --components 2 --dv 1 --no-bary
```

```
Star                : 2234_LL-Aqr
Continuum S/N       : ~146 per pixel
BJD = 2455144.516777
Component 1: RV = -35.7701 ± 0.0124 km/s   (amp=0.0563, area=0.436, sigma=3.09 km/s)
Component 2: RV = 20.9465 ± 0.0190 km/s   (amp=0.0328, area=0.209, sigma=2.54 km/s)
RV error C1: Bayesian (posterior median, 16-84 %) -35.7701 -0.0125 +0.0123 km/s; profile fit of the smoothed BF -35.7640 ± 0.0157
RV error C2: Bayesian (posterior median, 16-84 %) 20.9465 -0.0192 +0.0188 km/s; profile fit of the smoothed BF 20.9498 ± 0.0245
Light ratio (C2/C1, from BF areas) ≈ 0.480
```

The RVs and errors are the posterior of the component profiles, fitted
through the BF design matrix to the spectrum ([section
9](#bayesian-velocities-and-errors)). `--bf-error fit` gives the profile
fit of the smoothed BF instead (−35.7640 ± 0.0157 and +20.9498 ± 0.0245
km/s). This spectrum has the HARPS detector gap (zero flux at
530.4–533.7 nm). The BF leaves it out, and the run says so: `BF: 1858 rows
on a zero-filled gap ... left out of the SVD`.

This is the eclipsing binary **LL Aqr** on 2009-11-09, one of the two systems
of the [benchmark report](README.md#benchmark). Graczyk et al. (2016, A&A
594, A92, Table 1) give **−35.991 ± 0.027** and **+20.699 ± 0.052** km/s for
this epoch, so both components land within 0.25 km/s of the published
velocities. `--dv 1` sets a 1 km/s velocity step: these components are
3 km/s wide, and a step of several km/s would under-sample them ([section
9](#9-bf-velocity-step-and-the-reliability-of-the-rv-errors)). `--no-bary`
again — HARPS DRS products are barycentric too.

### `todcor` — an SB2 whose peaks have merged

```bash
python rv_analysis.py todcor --spectrum examples/example_TODCOR_NeoNarval_norm.txt \
    --template1 examples/template_5215K_Logg3.txt \
    --spectrograph NARVAL --dv 1
```

```
Star                : HD_105637
Grid                : 5685.7-6494.6 A, 39881 px, 1 km/s/px, |v| <= 200 km/s
Light ratio alpha   : 0.8676 (fitted, +-0.1069)
Peak correlation    : 0.1050
Detection gain      : 1.304   (one star gives 1.00; near-equal light owes ~1.3, so this is the test that matters here)
Component 1: RV = 44.2293 ± 1.3708 km/s
Component 2: RV = 22.3370 ± 2.6744 km/s
RV error C1: formal 0.6976 km/s; chunks: 1.3708 km/s (K = 5 of 5, q = 1.21, tau = 2.0630 km/s); adopted 1.3708 km/s = max(formal, chunks)
RV error C2: formal 1.3035 km/s; chunks: 2.6744 km/s (K = 5 of 5, q = 1.29, tau = 4.4083 km/s); adopted 2.6744 km/s = max(formal, chunks)
Brighter    : component 1 (RV = 44.23 km/s), by a factor 1.15 in flux
v_bary = -10.8108 km/s (applied)
```

The errors are the chunk errors: the five wavelength chunks disagree by
2–4 km/s (tau), more than the formal 2-D width error allows.

**Why this file is not a `bf` example.** The two components are 22 km/s
apart — closer than one profile FWHM — so their BF peaks have merged into a
single hump. `bf --components 2 --dv 1 --no-bary` on the very same spectrum
does not fail quietly, it says so:

```
Component 1: RV = 223.1873 ± 172.3032 km/s   (amp=0.0032, area=0.727, sigma=90.48 km/s)
Component 2: RV =  45.1560 ±   2.5125 km/s   (amp=0.0096, area=0.422, sigma=17.50 km/s)
Blended / degenerate fit: C1 has an RV error of 30.8 km/s. The multi-component
split is not constrained by the data. Single-component fit of the same profile:
RV = 43.0611 +/- 3.1051 km/s, sigma = 13.14 km/s.
WARNING: the fitted peak is only 2.3x the profile noise - this result is
probably NOT meaningful.
```

One component wanders off to +223 km/s as a 90 km/s-wide bump with an
error of 172 km/s: the split is not in the data, whatever seeds you give
it, and the single-component fit of the same profile is the honest reading
of it.
TODCOR models the blend instead of fitting around it, and returns both
velocities ([section 5](#todcor--two-dimensional-correlation-blended-sb2s)).
Note that here the barycentric correction **is** applied and no `--no-bary`
appears: Neo-Narval `st3` spectra are topocentric, and the tool decides that
from the header rather than from a flag.

Before trusting those velocities, check what the template alone can do —
noiseless composites, no spectrum needed:

```bash
python rv_analysis.py todcor --simulate \
    --template1 examples/template_5215K_Logg3.txt \
    --sim-sep 10 20 40 --spectrograph NARVAL
```

```
 sep_true   v1_rec   v2_rec   bias1   bias2  sep_err   alpha  swap
     10.0     0.00    10.00   +0.00   -0.00    -0.01   0.600     -
     20.0     0.00    20.00   +0.00   -0.00    -0.00   0.601     -
     40.0     0.00    40.00   +0.00   -0.00    -0.00   0.601     -
```

Bias ≤ 0.01 km/s and α recovered to 0.001 at every separation: the template
and the grid are not what limits this measurement.

On an ordinary laptop each run takes from a few seconds to about a minute.
Each writes `result_*.txt`, `result_*.png`, a line-check figure and the spectrum
shifted to the star's rest frame (`result_*_shifted*.fits`) into the current
directory, plus the run log ([section 11](#11-outputs)).

A self-test on synthetic data (no input files needed at all):

```bash
python rv_analysis.py demo --plot demo.png
```

Nothing to type? Run it with no arguments for the interactive mode:

```bash
python rv_analysis.py
```

### One file, nothing beside it

`rv_analysis.py` is the whole program. Every calculation is inside it, the
TODCOR engine included: copy that one file next to your spectra and every
command works.

The TODCOR engine sits at the end of the file, under the heading **TODCOR
engine**, between two `---8<---` markers. Those lines are the upstream
engine, unchanged, with its MIT licence. Do not edit them.

### The usual workflow

1. **Look at the header** — `header` tells you the object, the date, the
   coordinates and the mid-exposure BJD. In the GUI the same button also
   plots the spectrum in a zoomable window, so you can see what you are
   about to measure.
2. **Check the barycentric frame** — `barycheck` decides whether the
   wavelengths are already barycentric, so you know whether to pass
   `--no-bary` ([section 7](#7-barycentric-frame-what-the-headers-say-and-who-decides)).
3. **Normalize** — `normalize` (or `batch --normalize`, or the GUI batch's
   own tick box) if the spectrum is not yet continuum-normalized.
   Templates must be normalized too.
4. **Measure** — `ccf` or `bf` on one spectrum; inspect `result_BF.png`.
5. **Refine** — rerun with `--guess/--guess-amp/--guess-sigma`, `--dv`,
   `--profile rot`, or a wider `--vel-range` if the first fit is poor.
6. **Run the series** — over all epochs, with the settings you validated on
   the single spectrum. Two ways, same measurement:
   [**Batch...** in the GUI](#batch-a-whole-series-in-one-run) when you want
   to look at every fit and choose which epochs to keep, and the
   command-line [`batch`](#batch--a-time-series-into-an-rv-curve) when you
   want it unattended.
7. **Check the series** — `wilson` plots RV2 against RV1. For a real SB2 the
   points fall on a straight line whose slope is the mass ratio, so a
   mis-identified pair stands out at once and the primary is identified
   without an orbit.
8. **Solve the orbit** — `orbit` folds the same velocities on time, finds
   any swapped epochs against the orbit itself, and adds P, e, ω, K₁, K₂ and
   the minimum masses, each with errors from an emcee posterior (median and
   16th/84th percentiles).

The whole chain, which is the order the analysis is meant to run in:

```
   spectra  ->  TODCOR / BF  ->  RV1, RV2  ->  orbit             (§5 orbit)
   (§3)         (§5)             per epoch
                                    |
                                    +------->  Wilson plot: q, γ  (§5 wilson)
```

Each arrow is checkable on its own, and that is the point of splitting it
this way. The Wilson plot assumes **no orbit at all** and the orbit does not
use it, so the mass ratio it returns is an independent check on the one the
orbit fit produces: if the two disagree, the period is wrong. The same division
of labour is what
[MESS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026MNRAS.546.....N) (Nachmani,
Faigler & Mazeh 2026, MNRAS 546) uses — it extends TODCOR to a global
multi-epoch fit and then leans on "the Wilson relation between the two RVs
to infer the mass ratio and systemic velocity **without a full orbital
solution**" — and what Kovalev & Straumit
([2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stab3365), MNRAS 510, 1515) did for
the double-lined binaries of the open cluster M11: Wilson plot first, then
circular orbits fitted to the same velocities.

## 3. Preparing your data

### Observed spectrum

ASCII tables (`.txt`, `.dat`, `.obs`, `.nrm`, ...; first two numeric columns
are wavelength — Å or nm, auto-detected — and flux; comment lines, header
lines and `-` placeholders are skipped; a file with no header at all is read
just the same, and if it follows the DAO naming convention its metadata is
taken from the [file name](#metadata-in-the-file-name)) and the common
FITS layouts:

- ESPRESSO S2D (flux ext 1, wavelength ext 4),
- phase-3 style binary tables with WAVE/WAVELENGTH/LAMBDA/AWAV and
  FLUX/FLUX_REDUCED/INTENSITY/FLUX_NOR columns (FEROS, HARPS, UVES,
  PolarBase `.fts`, ...); the wavelength unit comes from the column's
  `TUNIT` (nm and µm are converted to Å), and a column with no unit and a
  median below 1500 is taken to be nm, as for ASCII,
- IRAF echelle **multispec** images (`CTYPE1 = 'MULTISPE'`, WAT2 cards;
  linear, log and Chebyshev/Legendre dispersion solutions, per-order
  Doppler factor applied),
- 1D images with a linear wavelength WCS (CRVAL1/CDELT1), including
  multi-extension products with one order per extension (e.g. SALT HRS),
- 2D/3D/4D images with a linear wavelength WCS along NAXIS1: degenerate
  axes (e.g. UVES/MIDAS `(1, 1, N)` products) are squeezed away and the
  IRAF band axis of onedspec/apall products (`BANDID1 = 'spectrum - ...'`,
  `'raw'`, `'background'`, `'sigma'`) is reduced to the *spectrum* band.

A file is read as FITS when its name ends in `.fits`, `.fit` or `.fts`
(each also `.gz`); anything else goes to the ASCII reader.

> **PolarBase (ESPaDOnS / NARVAL) downloads.** Each exposure comes as two
> products, `<odometer>i` (normalized by the pipeline) and `<odometer>i_u`
> (not normalized). Each product comes as a `.s` table and a `.fts` VO FITS
> file with the same data, plus a `.meta` and a pipeline log `.out`; the
> normalized product also has an LSD profile (`.lsd`). What to know:
>
> - **Use the `.fts` or the `.s`.** The `.fts` carries its own header. The
>   `.s` has none: its target, coordinates, epoch and exposure time are read
>   from the `.meta` beside it, and so are those of a window you cut out of
>   it, as long as the cut stays in the folder with its `.meta`. A copy moved
>   to another folder has no epoch unless you normalize it first, because
>   `normalize` writes everything into the copy's header.
> - **The wavelengths are already heliocentric**: run with `--no-bary`. The
>   small helio→barycentric term is then added for you.
> - **Use one product for every epoch.** The `i` and `i_u` wavelength scales
>   of one exposure differ by ~0.15 km/s, and `batch` warns when a queue
>   mixes them. Prefer `i`: the `_u` flux still carries the blaze, so it has
>   to be normalized before it can be measured.
>
> The details, and how each was measured, are in
> [section 7](#7-barycentric-frame-what-the-headers-say-and-who-decides).

> **Correlation functions, not spectra.** An ESO-MIDAS `xcorr` product
> (`m..._ln_corr_v.fits`) is the *output* of a cross-correlation: its
> `CRVAL1/CDELT1` axis is velocity in km/s, symmetric about zero (e.g.
> `CRVAL1 = -31515.9`, `CDELT1 = 1.92358`, 32768 pixels), and the RVs are
> simply the positions of its peaks. There is no spectrum inside it to
> correlate again, so `ccf`, `bf` and `todcor` refuse it — read as
> wavelengths, that axis is negative and every RV that follows is
> meaningless. Run them on the merged, normalized spectrum that went
> *into* the correlation instead.

> **Neo-Narval `st2`/`st3` files carry four exposures.** A level-2/3
> Libre-ESpRIT product is the combination of **four** level-1 exposures
> (the polarimetric sequence), and it keeps the file name *and the primary
> header* of the first of them — so `DATE-OBS` is a bare date,
> `TIME-ISO`/`TIMEEND` and `EXPTIME` describe sub-exposure 1 alone, and
> `DATE-FIT` is the file *creation* time, not an exposure time (NeoNarval
> data format, A. López Ariste, sect. 1). Taking the epoch from those cards
> puts the measurement half a sequence too early: **+15.8 min** on a
> 4×616 s sequence, +3.5 min on a 4×130 s one, which goes straight into the
> orbital phase and, for a short-period binary, into the RV itself.
>
> RVCalPy rebuilds the epoch from the **exposure-meter table** (the
> `Time Photometry`/`Photometry` extension), which is the one part of the
> file that spans the whole sequence, and uses its flux-weighted mid-time;
> the `FILE1..FILE4` cards plus `EXPTIME` are the fallback, and
> `TIME-ISO` + `EXPTIME`/2 the last resort. Which one was used is printed:
>
> ```
> Neo-Narval: epoch = mid of the 4-exposure sequence (exposure meter, flux-weighted, span 2518 s) = 2025-06-18T21:13:24.837, +15.8 min from the first sub-exposure
> ```
>
> `EXPTIME` is then reported as 0 on purpose — the epoch *is* the
> mid-time, so nothing further may be added to it.

> **A Neo-Narval exposure with no continuum solution is not a format
> error.** The `Intensity` column of an `st2`/`st3` file is already
> continuum-normalized; when the DRS finds no stellar continuum to divide
> by, it clips that column to the −1 / 10 bounds and leaves `Continuum` at
> its 2 ADU floor. Orders like that are dropped (a `Note:` line says how
> many), and if *every* order is like that the file is reported for what it
> is:
>
> ```
> recognized as a Libre-ESpRIT product (NARVAL / Neo-Narval / ESPaDOnS), but none
> of its 41 echelle orders has a usable continuum solution: 80% of the
> normalized-intensity pixels sit on the -1 / 10 clip bounds and the 'Continuum'
> column sits at its floor (median 2 ADU) ... this is an exposure to drop, not a
> format problem.
> ```
>
> The flux cannot be recovered from such a file (Intensity × Continuum is
> the clipped value times the floor): use another epoch, or have the
> level-1/level-2 data re-reduced. Note also that `normalize` is not needed
> for these files at all — `ccf`, `bf` and `todcor` read them directly.

> **Raw (unreduced) frames.** Files like `HARPS.2012-...fits` or
> `FEROS.2011-...fits` downloaded from the ESO *raw* archive are CCD
> exposures, not spectra; no RV can be measured from them. The loader
> recognizes them by their `ESO DPR` header cards and says so explicitly,
> instead of the generic "unrecognized FITS layout" error. Fetch their
> pipeline-reduced counterparts with [`fetch-adp`](#fetch-adp--download-reduced-eso-spectra)
> and analyze those instead.

### Template spectrum

An ASCII/`.prf` synthetic spectrum (synth3 / SynthV style), a FITS spectrum
(an observed standard used as template), or a PHOENIX model downloaded
automatically via `--teff/--logg/--feh` (needs `expecto`). **Templates must be
continuum-normalized, like the observed spectrum.**

A synth3/SynthV file may be given as it comes out of the code, with its line
list still at the top: the reader skips that block and reads the wavelength
table below it. That is what `examples/template_5215K_Logg3.txt` is.

The template only has to overlap the observed spectrum in wavelength — it may
be much shorter, and the analysis is restricted to the common range
automatically. The bundled template covers 4500-6500 A and is used against
spectra reaching from 378 to 919 nm.

Match the spectral type where you can, but do not overrate it: the same
5215 K template measures all three example stars in
[section 2](#2-quick-start), including one about 900 K hotter, and still
reproduces the published LL Aqr velocities to 0.25 km/s. A mismatch shows up
in the line-check figure, in the BF/CCF peak shape and — most of all — in
TODCOR's fitted alpha, long before it moves the velocity. In the line check
it looks like lines at the right place with the wrong strength. For a B star
measured with a template that is too cool, the He I lines come out deeper
than the model and the Si II / Fe II lines weaker, while the Balmer lines
still match ([Outputs](#11-outputs)).

### Observation time

ISOT (`2024-12-03T02:30:00`), a JD/BJD number (full or reduced by 2 400 000
— see [Julian dates](#julian-dates-full-or-reduced)), the old FITS date
convention `DD/MM/YY` (combined automatically with the `UT`/`UTMIDDLE` header
card when only a date is present), or, for a file with no header, the
[file name](#metadata-in-the-file-name), or, for a PolarBase `.s`, its
`.meta` file. PolarBase times are exposure **starts**: the `.meta`
`Observation date`, and also the `.fts` `DATE-OBS`, although its comment
calls it the middle. Half the exposure is added to both. Note that SOPHIE/HARPS-style `s1d` products are
already in the barycentric frame — do not apply the correction twice (see
[section 7](#7-barycentric-frame-what-the-headers-say-and-who-decides)).

**The exposure start comes from `MJD-OBS` when `DATE-OBS` is not enough.**
Some headers carry only a date in `DATE-OBS` — the 1998–99 FEROS-DRS files
write `DATE-OBS = 16/10/98`, the OM products `1999-07-15`. Read alone, that is
00:00 UT, and the BJD, the orbital phase and v_bary would belong to the wrong
hour of the night (up to 8.5 h and 0.6 km/s for those FEROS files). So, in
order:

1. a full timestamp in `DATE-OBS` is used as it is;
2. a date-only `DATE-OBS` takes its time from a full `DATE-BEG`/`DATE-FIT`,
   or from a `UT`/`TIME-OBS`-style card;
3. failing those, from **`MJD-OBS`**, the exposure start as a number (ESO,
   CFHT, the FEROS DRS), or from the ESO-MIDAS `TM-START` (seconds after 0 UT);
4. when a full `DATE-OBS` and `MJD-OBS` disagree by more than a second,
   `MJD-OBS` wins.

The run says which it used, on the line under `DATE-OBS = ...`, e.g.
*Exposure start: MJD-OBS = 51102.0736458 (01:46:02 UT); DATE-OBS (16/10/98)
has no time of day*. For ESO phase-3 products (HARPS, FEROS, UVES) the two
cards agree to the millisecond and nothing changes. The instrument rules
that know better still come first: the Neo-Narval sequence mid-time, the
SOPHIE `OHP OBS DATE START` card and the PolarBase `.meta`.

**Every epoch is the middle of the exposure.** Whatever card gives the start,
the BJD, v_bary and the orbital phase are computed for the middle of the
exposure:

| Source of the time | What the time is | Added by RVCalPy |
|---|---|---|
| ESO phase-3 (HARPS, FEROS, UVES), `DATE-OBS` or `MJD-OBS` | start | + EXPTIME/2 |
| SOPHIE `OHP OBS DATE START` | start | + `OHP CCD DKTM`/2 (the DRS's own BJD agrees within about a second, after converting its UTC time to TDB) |
| PolarBase `.meta` / `.fts` | start | + EXPTIME/2 |
| Neo-Narval sequence | middle of the 4-exposure sequence (exposure meter) | nothing (EXPTIME is set to 0) |
| DAO, `JD...` in the file name | middle | nothing (EXPTIME is set to 0); `DO...` is the start |
| a JD/BJD number (`--bjd`, `--obstime 2461...`) | taken as a mid-exposure BJD | nothing |

A calendar time with **no exposure time** anywhere cannot be moved to the
middle. RVCalPy then uses it as given and says so —
*WARNING: no exposure time (EXPTIME) is known for this spectrum ...* — on the
screen, in the result file and in the report. Give the middle of the
exposure as a BJD in that case.

A normalized copy is written with the full start time. A copy that
holds only a date takes the time from the file it was normalized from, when
that file is still where the copy says; if it is not, the run prints — and
writes into the result — *WARNING: the observation time '1999-07-25' is a
date with no time of day ...*. Re-normalize from the original, or give
`--obstime` (one spectrum) or `--bjd` (a batch).

**How the BJD_TDB is computed.** The BJD in every result is computed by
RVCalPy, not copied from the header:

1. **Mid-exposure time (UTC).** For Neo-Narval, the flux-weighted mean time
   of the exposure-meter data stored in the file (HDU 4, `Time Photometry`
   and `Photometry`). The header's Julian date (`DATE_JUL`) is not used,
   because it is only the start of the first of the four sub-exposures
   combined in each spectrum. For SOPHIE, half the exposure time
   (`OHP CCD DKTM` / 2) is added to the start time (`OHP OBS DATE START`).
2. **Conversion to BJD_TDB (astropy).**

       BJD_TDB = JD_UTC(mid) + (TDB − UTC) + light-travel time to the solar-system barycentre

   The light-travel time uses the star's RA/Dec from the header and the
   observatory position (TBL from the Neo-Narval header, OHP for SOPHIE).
3. **Validation against the pipelines.** The SOPHIE results agree with the
   DRS BJD (`OHP DRS BJD`) to within about a second, once the DRS's UTC time
   scale is converted to TDB. The Neo-Narval results are a few minutes later
   than `BTLA`, the pipeline's barycentric time at the start of the
   sequence. This is expected, because RVCalPy uses the mid-sequence time
   instead of the start time. The barycentric correction itself (`BTLA`
   minus the start time, compared with RVCalPy's BJD minus the mid time)
   agrees to within about a second. Every run prints both checks as
   `cross-check:` lines (see
   [section 7](#7-barycentric-frame-what-the-headers-say-and-who-decides)).

## 4. Interactive mode

```bash
python rv_analysis.py          # same as: python rv_analysis.py gui
```

The windows have a light, flat look: light-grey window, white fields and
selected tabs, thin grey borders.

**Display scale.** The GUI follows the desktop's display scale and text
size. It reads `Xft.dpi` (the value GNOME gives X11 programs, also in a
Wayland session) and sizes its text, check boxes, graphs and windows by
it, at start and again within 2 s when the scale changes. Windows that are
already open keep their size; a new window opens at the new scale. When
the desktop gives no `Xft.dpi`, the standard sizes are used;
`RVCALPY_UI_SCALE=1.5 python rv_analysis.py` sets a scale by hand.

**File and folder choosers** (every **Browse...**, Save, Add a folder...)
keep their size while you move between folders. A chooser opens at
820 × 520 pixels (× the display scale), and then at the size it had when
it was last closed, so a size you drag it to stays.

The main window (about 790 × 610 pixels) has everything on one page: each
section is a title line over its groups, side by side, and one row of
small buttons is at the very bottom:

- **Spectrum** — **Files** (Spectrum and Template, each with
  **Browse...**, and Template 2 for TODCOR; under a line **Normalize
  raw...**, **Header** and **Spectrum**), **Instrument** (Spectrograph,
  Resolution (R), vsini [km/s] with the smallest resolvable v sin i ≈ c/R
  under it, then
  the wavelength **Range [nm]**, empty for the full range) and **Method**
  (a large **Method...** button with the summary of what is set under it);
- **Target**, right under it — three groups laid out alike, fields at the
  top and the group's buttons under a line at the bottom, the buttons of
  all three on one row: **Identifier** (Name, RA, Dec, and after a lookup the
  spectral type and one magnitude — V, or for a star SIMBAD has no V for,
  the first of G, R, B, K it has; button **SIMBAD**), **Observation** (Obs
  time — ISOT, BJD or DD/MM/YY — and Observatory; button **Custom
  observatory...**) and **Ephemeris** (T0 with its unit, Period in days;
  buttons **VarAstro** and **HJD -> BJD**), and under the three, on a row
  of its own, the **Apply barycentric correction** box with, on the same
  line, what the spectrum's header claims about its frame.
  It is optional, for the barycentric correction, and **Header fills it
  from the spectrum's own header** — the two sections sit one above the
  other so that what the file says is seen landing in these fields, and
  the blanks the header could not fill are the ones left to type or to
  fetch with SIMBAD;
- the bottom row: **Batch...**, **Run**, **Save**, then after a short
  line **Wilson plot...** and **Orbit...**, which work on a finished table
  of velocities, not on a spectrum.

The window follows its contents: it grows a row when TODCOR adds
Template 2.

Check boxes are round, like the radio buttons of the method choice, with
a dot when ticked.

Resolve the target via SIMBAD (or enter RA/Dec), pick
the normalized observed spectrum and the template, and choose the method via
the **Method...** button — like **Normalize raw...**, it opens a small
sub-window offering **CCF (Cross Correlation)**, **TODCOR (CCF-SB2)** or
**BF (Broadening)**, in that order. Each method's panel lists one setting per
line — name, box, unit or a grey hint — and the line under **Method...** in the
main window repeats what is set (e.g. `BF, 2 component(s), window ±500 km/s,
Gaussian, automatic component search`).

Choosing **BF** brings up **Velocity window ±** (km/s), **Components** (1 = SB1,
2 = SB2, 3 = SB3) and **Fit profile** (Gaussian, or the Gray rotational profile,
whose fitted width is *v* sin *i* — give R), then a **Component search** box with
two options:

- **Automatic component search** — no input needed; every assumption field
  is greyed out and the components are found by the iterative
  fit-and-subtract search.
- **With assumptions** — press **Show smoothed BF** to display, with one
  click, only the smoothed BF (no Gaussians fitted yet); read each
  component's approximate RV (x-axis), Amp (y-axis) and width off the curve
  and type them into **RVs / Amps / Sigmas**. Write one value per component,
  **largest Gaussian first, smallest last** — this order sets the C1/C2/...
  labels. The fit then starts from your estimates, exactly like the
  reference notebook.

The systemic γ and the orbit's e and ω for the phase-based C1/C2 labels
([section 10](#10-component-labels)) are not in the panel; give them on
the command line (`--gamma`, `--ecc`, `--omega`).

Choosing **CCF** brings up **Correlation mode** (**Line mask**, safe for hot
stars, or **Full template**), **RV min** / **RV max** (km/s; leave either
empty for the automatic two-stage ±900 km/s search), **RV step** (the fine
grid, km/s) and **Exclude hydrogen lines**. The hydrogen box only acts on the
line mask, so it is greyed out under *Full template*. The spectrum is
always cleaned before the correlation ([Cleaning and the RV
error](#cleaning-and-the-rv-error)); only the command line has
`--no-clean`.

Choosing **TODCOR** is for an SB2 whose two peaks blend. It reveals a
third file row in the **Files** group, right under Template — **Template
2**, the secondary's template, with the grey hint *empty: same as template
1*; Template is then the primary's. Its **TODCOR settings** panel lists one setting
per line: **Velocity step** (km/s per pixel of the log-λ grid, `--dv`),
**Velocity window ±** (the search half-width, `--max-rv`), **Light ratio
α (F2/F1)** (empty = fitted, `--alpha`) and **vsini of template 2**
(`--vsini2`; template 1's is the *v* sin *i* box in the main window), then
**Clean the observed spectrum first** (on by default: per-order spike
rejection and continuum re-flattening). **vsini of template 2 copies the
main window's vsini**: each value you type in the main window
appears in the box as well, so both templates get the same rotational
broadening. To give template 2 a different value, type it in the box; the
box then keeps your value. Type 0 for no rotation of template 2. Clear the
box, or type the same value as the main window, and it copies the main
window's vsini again. An empty box at the run also means "the same as
template 1". The report shows the two values in its `Template vsini` line,
and the batch window in its **vsini of template 2** row. Below them, in a box of its own,
**Template test — no spectrum needed**: *Run the self-calibration instead of
measuring* measures the method's own bias on noiseless composites built from
the two templates, at the **Separations** you list (that box is greyed out
until the test is ticked). Run it first.

The panel itself is deliberately bare — what the method is, why the cleaning
matters, and how to read a fitted α are all documented in
[`todcor`](#todcor--two-dimensional-correlation-blended-sb2s) rather than
repeated on the widget.

**Leave Template 2 empty unless the two stars differ in spectral type** —
template 1 is then used for both components, which is the normal setup for a
twin binary and keeps the correlation surface exactly symmetric. Filling it
with a second, near-identical template is the one choice to avoid; see
[One template or two?](#one-template-or-two).

A file whose WCS axis is a velocity axis is not a spectrum but the *output*
of a correlation, and no method here can use it. You do not have to spot
this yourself: an orange note appears beside the file as soon as it is
selected, and **Run** stops with the same explanation.

Press **Run** in the main window: the result opens in a window of its own,
**RV result**, beside the main window — the main window stays the form it
is. The fit is shown with one dashed profile per component, labelled `C1:
amp = ..., RV = ... km/s` in the legend — `amp` is the peak height read off
the BF axis (the profile integrals/areas and the light ratios stay in the
text summary). The legend is shown on screen and in the saved
`result_BF.png` / `result_CCF.png`; untick **Figure options... → Legend
shown** to close it, and the PNG then matches the screen. **Nothing is written to disk until you press Save** — in the
result window, or beside Run.

In the RV result window the report sits above the figure, the two sharing the
height through a **draggable sash**: drag it down to read a long report in
full, up to see the figure large. The report does not wrap lines — the epoch
tables are column-aligned — and has its own scrollbars. The next Run (or
Wilson plot) reuses the same window.

**Every graph has the same look:** observed velocities in royal blue (star 1) and maroon (star
2), as small plain dots; the orbit's model curves in light steel blue
(star 1) and light coral (star 2); a black dashed line for γ and a black zero line under
the residuals; no grid. Every colour can be changed in **Figure options...**
(Curves tab — each colour is a drop-down list of colour names, like Line
style, showing the colour's name, `royalblue`, `lightcoral`, or its hex
value; any name or hex can also be typed in, and **...** opens a colour
picker). The measured
**profiles are grey** — the BF (raw light, smoothed thick) and the CCF;
TODCOR's two cuts are thin black — and their **peaks look
the same in every method**: component 1 (RV1) royal blue, component 2 (RV2)
maroon (a third green) — the colours of the two stars' points on the orbit's
Solution Curve and the RV curve — each fitted peak dashed and its RV a dotted
vertical line in the same colour (TODCOR: the dotted v1 and v2 lines), the
total fit thin black. The orbit's **Solution Curve**
draws every velocity with its **error bar**, on the curve and in the
residual panel, the repeated points included; the other velocity plots
(RV curve, Wilson plot) carry no error bars — their errors are in the
hover boxes and the result tables. A curve folded on the
period runs from phase −0.25 to 1.25, every point drawn once more one cycle
to either side, and the orbit fit puts its residual panel straight under the
data at 1.5 : 1, with no gap. Tick labels never use an offset (`+2.455e6`):
a BJD axis shows the whole number. Every graph opens **with its legend**,
and it stays until you close it with **Figure options... → Legend shown**.
**Save** writes the graph as you see it: a legend you closed is not in the
PNG either. The BF/CCF legends quote the measured RVs, as described below.

Under every graph — the RV result, the orbit window's pages, the batch
detail window, **RV curve...** and **Spectrum** — comes a toolbar
(matplotlib's Qt toolbar): **Home**, **Back**,
**Forward** | **Pan**, **Zoom** | **Subplots**, **Customize** | **Save**,
and on its right the **cursor position**, named after the axes — `Phase =
0.523   RV [km/s] = -12.31`, with the precision the current zoom needs (a
panel without a label of its own, such as the orbit fit's upper panel,
takes the label of the panel it shares the axis with; over the TODCOR
surface the correlation under the cursor follows on a second line). Pan:
left button drags, right button zooms; Zoom: a rectangle; x or y held down
fixes that axis. Under the toolbar is a row of three buttons:

| Control | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Pop Plot Window** | A copy of the graph in a window of its own, with its own toolbar, row, menu and hover boxes. The copy stays open beside the next result, and zooming or editing it leaves the original as it was |

Lines drawn across a whole panel (γ, zero) are left out — their x is not data. On a folded curve the repeated points are exported too |

The toolbar's **Save** and **Save figure as...** write the graph as it is

the extension typed (PNG when none is). The dialog suggests a name — on
the Spectrum window e.g. `TYC_3429-458-1_Mg_I_b_triplet_C1.png` — and
opens in the folder the last figure was saved to.

The menu stays out of the way while the toolbar's pan or zoom tool is
active, since those use the right button themselves. Changes made this way
are on screen and in **Save figure as...**; the Save button writes the
result's figures as the program drew them, at the size they were made
with — never at the size of the window they were shown in.

**Save** asks two things. First **which files**: a small window lists every
file this result can produce, each with a tick box and its name —

- **Result report** — `result_<METHOD>.txt`;
- **Figure** and **Line-check figure** — `result_<METHOD>.png`,
  `result_<METHOD>_linecheck.png`;
- **Profile table: RV vs CCF / BF / TODCOR** — `result_<METHOD>_profile.txt`,
  the measured profile as plain columns, to plot it with any other program
  (see [Outputs](#11-outputs));
- **Rest-frame spectrum** — `result_<METHOD>_shifted.fits`, one `_C1`/`_C2`
  file per component;
- **Corner plot (orbit posterior)**, **Wilson plot (orbit)**,
  **Posterior table: orbit parameters** and **RV table: total errors and
  residuals (orbit)** — `result_orbit_corner.png`,
  `result_orbit_wilson.png`, `result_orbit_posterior.txt` and
  `result_orbit_rv.txt`, offered only for an orbit result (see
  [Reading the corner plot](#reading-the-corner-plot) and
  [The model error and the total RV error](#the-model-error-and-the-total-rv-error)).

All are ticked the first time; afterwards the window opens with your last
choice, and a choice it has not offered before (the corner plot, the first
time an orbit is saved) starts ticked. Under the tick boxes, **Confidence**
sets the confidence level of intervals written beside the
1σ errors: `1 sigma (68.27 %)` (the default — nothing is added), `90 %`,
`95 %`, `2 sigma (95.45 %)`, `99 %`, `3 sigma (99.73 %)`, or a level you
type (`0.997`, `99.7`, `3 sigma`). It is chosen here, after the
analysis: the fit and the 1σ errors do not change, only what the saved
files show. See [Confidence level of the saved
errors](#confidence-level-of-the-saved-errors). **Save all
kept...** of a batch has the same field, for every epoch's report and the
combined RV curve and orbit files. Then **where**: a directory chooser opens on the folder the run would
have used, and the ticked files are written there under their usual names.
Cancelling either window writes nothing at all. The RV result window then
lists the paths the files actually went to. A Wilson result offers its report and
figure the same way; an orbit result its report, orbit figure, corner plot and
posterior table. On the command line each command writes every file,
where its `--output` and `--plot` say, immediately
after the run (the profile table included).

Other GUI features:

- **Batch...** — the same settings applied to a whole series of spectra, with
  every fit shown at once afterwards. It has [two sections of its
  own](#batch-a-whole-series-in-one-run) below.
- **Normalize raw...** — continuum-normalize **one** raw spectrum, with a
  preview: choose the continuum model (a cubic **B-spline** with knots
  every window — the one selected when the window opens — or **Polynomial**
  in blended windows; the **Polynomial order** box appears only when
  Polynomial is chosen, as Template 2 appears only for TODCOR, and the
  clipping **Iterations** apply to both),
  press
  **Preview** to overlay the result on the template, and adjust until they
  match. This is the place to settle the continuum on one file; the batch
  normalizes a whole queue with a fixed B-spline and no preview.
- **Header** — opens the **Header** window of the selected file, with two
  side tabs: **Cards**, the header exactly as it is written in the file
  (every HDU of a FITS file, each under a `--- HDU k: NAME (type) ---` line;
  for an ASCII table its comment header and, for PolarBase, the `.meta`
  sidecar), and **Summary**, the [standard summary
  block](#the-standard-summary-block) — what the program reads from those
  cards. It also fills the Target fields from **the file that is loaded
  now**. Each press opens a window of its own, so two files' headers can be
  compared side by side.

  Load a different spectrum and the fields the program filled in for the
  previous one are cleared the moment the path changes — including **T0 and
  Period**, which belong to a target rather than to a file and so are the
  easiest to leave behind: an ephemeris fetched for the last star will
  otherwise still be sitting there, folding the next star's velocities on
  somebody else's period. Press **Header** again and the box describes the
  new spectrum.

  Values *you* typed are not cleared, because you put them there on purpose.
  They are kept, listed in a note when the spectrum changes, and if the new
  file disagrees with one, `Header` says so — with both values — instead of
  overwriting it.
- **Spectrum** — plots the selected spectrum in a window of its own. The
  window carries the full matplotlib navigation bar, so it can be zoomed,
  panned, sent back **Home** and saved as a PNG, and the cursor position is
  read out in wavelength and flux as it moves — the same interactive look a
  MIDAS plot gives you — plus the Pop / Export / Grid row of every graph.
  One line is drawn per echelle order.

  What it draws is what the analysis will actually read, not the raw file:
  orders the reader discards are absent. On a Libre-ESpRIT `st3`, where
  roughly half the orders have no continuum solution at this S/N, the window
  therefore shows ~17 of the 41 orders. Use it as a check on what the fit
  will see.

  **Live line check.** Above the graph, four rows of controls let you lay
  the spectrum over the template at a line of your choice and mark where
  each star's lines fall — the two velocities checked by eye, without
  saving anything. The graph is
  redrawn after every change (a list choice, a tick, or Enter in a box);
  **Draw** does it by hand.

  - **Range** — *Whole spectrum* (the plot above), one of the strong lines
    — *Ca II H & K* (3919–3983 Å), *H gamma* (4310–4370 Å), *H beta*
    (4831–4891 Å), *Mg I b triplet* (5157.5–5193.5 Å), *H alpha*
    (6533–6593 Å) — or your own **From** / **to** in Å (press Enter; the
    list switches to *Custom*). The ranges are rest wavelengths; the line
    centres are marked with dotted lines. Pick a line the spectrum shows
    clearly: Ca II H & K and the Mg I b triplet in cool stars, the Balmer
    lines in hotter ones. The file's own coverage is printed beside the
    boxes.
  - **RV1** / **RV2** — the two stars' measured (uncorrected) velocities,
    filled from the last Run when it was a run of this same file (the
    line under the controls lists them), or for a `shifted` file its own
    star's RV from the file header (`RVSHIFT`); type any velocity in
    instead — from another run, a paper or an orbit's prediction — and
    press Enter. The spectrum is never moved: it is drawn as it is in the
    file. At every line centre of the range a dotted line marks where
    each star's line falls in it: **RV1** in royal blue, **RV2** in
    maroon (the component colours of every RVCalPy graph, as on the
    TODCOR figure), with their velocities in the legend. A star with no
    RV gets no line; with neither, the line centres are marked in grey.
    In a *Custom* range the centres are the known lines inside it, or
    else the template's deepest line.
  - **Template at** — *RV1*, *RV2* or *Rest*: where the template is
    drawn. A raw or normalized spectrum still carries the stars'
    velocities, so its lines are at λ₀ · (1 + RV/c), and the template has
    to be moved there to lie on them; switching between RV1 and RV2 shows
    which absorption belongs to which star. A `result_*_shifted_C<k>.fits`
    file is already in star k's rest frame — RVCalPy recognises it from
    its header (`SPECSYS = SOURCE`, `COMPON`, `RVSHIFT`) and says so
    beside the buttons — so there star k's lines are at rest and the
    other star's at its velocity relative to star k, about RV2 − RV1; the
    dotted lines and the template follow that. The window opens on *RV1*
    for a raw or normalized file and on *Rest* for a shifted one.
  - **Template** — draws the main window's **Template** (Template 1)
    over the spectrum, where **Template at** puts it.
    **Broaden** applies the main window's **Resolution** and **vsini**
    first, as the analysis does; **Normalize locally** divides the
    spectrum and the template, inside the range, by a straight line
    through their upper envelope, as the line-check fallback panels do.
    A template that does not cover the range is left out, and the window
    says so.
  - **Light fraction f** — dilutes the template to f·T + (1 − f) (e.g.
    f = 0.5 draws 0.5·T + 0.5), in steps of 0.05 with the arrows or any
    value typed in and Enter; 1.00, the default, leaves it as it is. In
    an SB2 each star's lines sit on the light of both stars, so a star
    giving the fraction f of the light shows lines only f times as deep
    as a single-star template. Lower f until the template's strong lines
    are as deep as the observed lines of the star it sits on: f is then
    a rough estimate of that star's share of the light (with *Template
    at RV2*, of the companion's; the two shares add up to about 1). It
    is a by-eye estimate: a template whose Teff or metallicity is off
    also changes the line depths, so weak lines that stay too deep at
    the f that fits the strong ones point to the template, not to f.
    The legend shows the value ("Template at RV1, f = 0.55").
  - The legend goes where it covers the fewest points (lower right for
    the whole spectrum). Where every place is crowded, drag it with the
    mouse (no Pan or Zoom tool on). Save writes it where you put it; a
    redraw puts it back to the free place.
  - **Observed** / **Template** — the colour of each curve (a CSS colour
    name from the list, or any matplotlib colour typed in, e.g. `#4286f4`;
    the defaults are a black observed spectrum and a crimson template),
    its style (*Line*, *Steps*, *Dots*, *Line + dots*) and its width. The
    choice is kept for the next Spectrum window of the session.
  - The flux axis of a range is scaled to the 0.5–99.5 % range of the
    observed flux, widened to the template's deepest line and to the
    continuum at 1, so a single cosmic-ray spike or dead pixel does not
    squeeze the lines into a strip. To set it yourself, use the toolbar's
    **Customize** (Figure options → Axes → Y-Axis Min / Max).
  - The window keeps one graph and redraws it in place, so **Save** always
    writes what is on the screen: untick **Template** and the saved file
    has no template. A zoom (toolbar or Customize) is kept when you tick
    the template on or off or change the colours, RVs or *Template at*;
    a new range, or Normalize locally switched, starts again from the
    whole range, and **Home** always goes back to it.

  The graph is titled with the star - the header's object, written one
  way (`TYC_3429-458-1` and `TYC 3429-458-1` both read *TYC 3429-458-1*,
  `2234_LL-Aqr` reads *LL Aqr*), else the main window's Target name -
  and the range; the file name stays in the window's title bar.

  To look for both stars of an SB2, take the epoch with the largest
  separation and a window of weak, sharp metal lines (the Mg I b and
  Balmer lines are too broad: a companion 1–1.5 Å away merges into their
  wings): an absorption feature should sit on each dotted line, and the
  template should lie on the one set with *Template at RV1* and on the
  other with *RV2*. A faint companion's lines are
  shallow, and at low S/N they may not be seen one by one even when the
  correlation detects the star.

  How to read it: each star's lines sit on its dotted line, and the
  template, moved to one of them, lies on that star's lines. In an
  SB2 both stars' lines are diluted by the other star's light, so the
  observed lines are shallower than the single-star template; that is
  expected, not a mismatch.
- **Wilson plot...** — in the bottom row, after the line that separates
  it from Batch... / Run / Save: it uses no spectrum, no
  template, no method and not the Run button — it works on a finished table
  of velocities. It gives the mass ratio from that RV series: pick a
  table and then **one column per axis**, chosen by **column number counted
  from 0** — `0`, `1`, `2` … , with the column's name shown beside the number
  when the file has a header (`3: ccf_rv1`). Nothing is assumed about the
  names: the velocities may be called anything, sit in either order, or come
  from different methods (`ccf_rv1` against `bf_rv2`), and a table with no
  header at all is selected from just the same way. Each axis has an error
  dropdown of its own. When the table names its columns, the dialog opens
  with the matching error column already chosen — `ccf_err1` for `ccf_rv1`,
  `RV1_err` for `RV1[km/s]`, `sigma_RV1` for
  `RV1`, and the other spellings listed under
  [`wilson`](#wilson--mass-ratio-from-the-rv1-vs-rv2-plot). Otherwise it
  shows `(auto)`, and a table with no error column is fitted unweighted.
  **Check the error dropdowns before comparing two runs**: the same table
  with and without its errors gives a different slope, rms and robust σ. The RV1-vs-RV2 line
  gives M₂/M₁, the systemic velocity and which component is the primary.
  **Swap mis-identified pairs** starts **unticked**: the pairs are fitted
  as the table gives them, and a mis-identified epoch shows up as an
  outlier (leave it out with a click, see below); tick it to have
  decidable swaps found and exchanged as `wilson` does by default on the
  command line. **Add v_bary** (unticked) adds each row's v_bary to both
  velocities. **Plot** shows it in the RV result
  window, with the right-click menu on the graph and, beside **Pop Plot
  Window**, a **Swap axes: V₁ ↔ V₂** button: it redraws the plot with V₂
  across and V₁ up (and back), axis names included. The fit and the report
  do not change, only the drawing: with V₁ across the legend's slope is
  −M₁/M₂ = −1/q, with V₂ across it is −M₂/M₁ = −q, the number in the title
  ([why](#wilson--mass-ratio-from-the-rv1-vs-rv2-plot)). A click on a
  point keeps the orientation chosen. The dialog has no star or dataset
  filter: it reads the whole table and **warns in the result** when the rows
  hold more than one star or more than one dataset. Those filters are still
  there on the command line, and a table covering several systems should be
  run there. See [`wilson`](#wilson--mass-ratio-from-the-rv1-vs-rv2-plot).
- **Orbit...** — beside **Wilson plot...**, the same table and column
  choice, plus an **Orbit** box: **Period** [d] (taken from the window's
  ephemeris when one is filled in; empty: the period is searched),
  **Prior width** (× the least-squares error, default 10), **Steps** (per
  walker, default 5000; 64 walkers), **Exclude** (a phase: the
  epochs closer than this to a conjunction are left out — eclipses; empty:
  none) and **Model error** (`additive`, the default, `night`, `scale` or
  `none`; see
  [The model error and the total RV error](#the-model-error-and-the-total-rv-error)),
  **Eccentric orbit**, **Posterior errors (emcee)** and **Third-star gamma
  (RV3)** (the binary's γ follows the third star — the table needs an RV3
  column; see [The third star](#the-third-star)). The orbit runs in its **basic mode**: there is no
  **Swap mis-identified pairs** and no **Add v_bary** here — the velocities
  go into the fit as the table gives them, in their order and frame, and
  no pair is exchanged. For a swap repair or v_bary added, use the main
  window's **Wilson plot...** (the detailed tool), or the `orbit` command,
  whose swap check is on by default. A swapped epoch left in shows at once
  — far off both curves, and as the outlier of the Wilson tab's residuals
  — and inflates the errors: with `Model error` = `scale`, LL Aqr's one
  blended epoch at conjunction raises the RV error scale from 1.8 to 16. **Fit orbit** runs the
  least-squares fit and the posterior, which takes about ten seconds, and
  opens the result in a window of its own with its pages as side tabs,
  captions written upwards: **Orbit fit** (the folded
  curve with the model of the posterior medians, and the residuals of the fit
  underneath; **hold the mouse over any point** — on the curve or in the
  residuals — and a box shows that epoch's name, BJD and phase, both
  velocities with their errors and residuals, the component under the cursor
  marked `>`, and whether the pair was swapped; the window opens on this
  page), **Corner plot**, **Wilson plot** and **Report** — the same report
  Save writes to `result_orbit.txt`, which ends with the Wilson plot's
  numbers. The **Wilson plot** tab is a basic one: the same pairs, in the
  order the orbit used them, with the fitted line, γ, q and the primary,
  and the residuals about the line beside it — no swap repair of its own,
  and it does not enter the orbit fit; the hover box gives each epoch's
  name, orbital phase and velocities. The window
  opens at about 1040 × 660 pixels; resize it freely — the orbit fit
  fills its page, and the corner and Wilson plots keep their shape. The PNGs are written
  at their own size whatever the window's.
  That window's **Save...** asks which of the five files to write (report,
  orbit figure, corner plot, Wilson plot, posterior table). See
  [`orbit`](#orbit--sb2-orbital-elements-the-last-step-of-the-chain).
- **VarAstro / HJD → BJD** — fetch the eclipsing-binary ephemeris (T0, P)
  from var.astro.cz and convert T0 to BJD_TDB for the orbital phase. The
  lookup handles VarAstro's GCVS spelling automatically (a name like
  `V563 Lyr` is retried as the zero-padded `V0563 Lyr`) and, if the name
  is still not found, retries the target's **SIMBAD cross-identifiers**,
  matching the returned star by label so a fuzzy near-name is not accepted.
- **How a star name is written does not matter.** SIMBAD, the coordinate
  lookup (`--object`) and VarAstro try the name as typed first and then its
  standard forms: `KX-Aqr`, `kx-aqr`, `KX_Aqr`, `KXAqr` and `kxaqr` all
  find **KX Aqr**, `v563-lyr` finds V563 Lyr, and an ESO header OBJECT with
  a programme number in front, such as `2234_LL-Aqr`, finds LL Aqr. A
  hyphen or underscore becomes a space only before a letter, so the sign
  in `BD-12 345` or a 2MASS `J…-…` name is never dropped (that would name a
  different star), and a variable-star designation gets its capitals back
  (`ll aqr` → `LL Aqr`).
- **The lookup is quick.** It goes through the **Sesame** name resolver
  (SIMBAD's own data), which answers in well under a second, with every
  spelling in one request — the likeliest one (`KX Aqr`) alone first, the
  rest only if that misses. SIMBAD's script service, which can take 15 s
  to a minute, is asked only when Sesame cannot be reached. Each request
  goes at the same time to the CDS hosts in Strasbourg and to the CfA
  mirrors at Harvard (`vizier.cfa.harvard.edu`, `simbad.cfa.harvard.edu`),
  and the first answer is used; a server that does not answer is left out
  for 10 min. A name found once is kept for the rest of the session. In
  the GUI the search runs in the background — *searching SIMBAD ...* shows
  under the fields and the window stays usable. When no server answers,
  the message says *SIMBAD not reachable* after about 8 s, and further
  lookups in the next 2 min fail at once: give RA/Dec by hand, or check
  the network.
- **Observatory** — the dropdown opens on **(from Header)**, which reads the position out of the selected spectrum and
  falls back to the default site only when the file gives none; the rest of
  the list is the built-in sites (ESO VLT/La Silla/NTT/ALMA/
  ELT, OHP (Saint-Michel), CTIO, Las Campanas, SOAR, Gemini, CFHT, Keck,
  LBT, TBL, TNG, TUG, DAG, Kreiken, …). The site coordinates stay stored behind
  the selection (not shown on the widget); an observatory not in the list is
  entered through the **Custom observatory...** button, which opens a small window for
  lon [°E], lat [°] and alt [m]. These coordinates drive the barycentric
  correction — including the diurnal (Earth-rotation) term — the way the
  MIDAS `comp/bary` command takes the observatory longitude and latitude
  explicitly.
- **RA / Dec** accept decimal degrees or sexagesimal (`12:50:39.7`,
  `-03:07:49.8`, or the MIDAS comma style `12,50,39.7`).
- The barycentric correction is **opt-in: the box starts unticked**.
  v_bary is always computed and reported, but only added to the
  RVs when you tick **Apply barycentric correction** yourself. The box is
  never ticked, unticked or disabled behind your back; when the selected
  spectrum's header claims a frame, the claim appears in grey beside the
  box (*header says already corrected — check first* / *header says
  topocentric*) and nothing else happens. So a run made
  straight after opening the window reports RVs in the **measured
  (observer) frame** — tick the box when you want them barycentric. **With the box ticked,
  every figure is drawn on the corrected velocity axis** (v → v + v_bary,
  relativistically): the smoothed BF or CCF, the fitted peaks, their RV
  lines and labels, and for TODCOR the surface's axes with its marked
  maximum, both cuts and the v1/v2 lines all show the **reported
  (barycentric)** values; the axis is labelled `(barycentric)` (TODCOR:
  `(bary.)`) and the BF/CCF legend adds `v_bary = … (applied)`. Unticked, the axis is the measured
  frame, which is then also the reported one. The fit itself is always
  made in the measured frame and never altered — shifting the axes moves
  every curve and its peak together. The result text file contains the
  star, the raw RV, the corrected RV and v_bary side by side, one line per
  component, and the profile table has both velocity axes (`RV` and
  `RV_raw`). TODCOR's line under the figure says which frame is plotted
  — `plotted = reported (barycentric)`, v_bary and the measured v1/v2.

### Batch: a whole series in one run

**Batch...**, next to Run and Save, measures many spectra with the settings
already on the window — one template, one method, one wavelength range, one
resolution, one component count — and puts every result in front of you at
once. It is the same thing as pressing Run ten times, without the ten
chances to change a setting halfway through: each epoch is measured by
exactly the `ccf`, `bf` or `todcor` call the single-spectrum Run would have
made on that file, so a batch result and a hand-run result on the same
spectrum are the same numbers.

Set the window up first — template, method, spectrograph, target — then
press **Batch...**. The dialog has a queue and a panel listing exactly what
the series will share, so there is nothing hidden:

- **Add spectra...** — multi-select, in one or several goes.
- **Add a folder...** — every spectrum file in a directory, with the
  template excluded, since a template queued as an epoch would be measured
  against itself.
- **Remove selected** / **Clear** — the queue is de-duplicated and sorted; a
  file queued twice would be one epoch entered twice in the RV curve, under
  the same BJD.

**Raw spectra are normalized first, in one pass.** Every method here
measures a *normalized* spectrum: it compares line depths against a
continuum at 1.0, and a raw frame still carrying the blaze has no such
continuum. Queueing raw frames and pressing **Run all** would measure the
blaze as if it were the star. So tick **Normalize the queued spectra
first** and the run asks for a directory of its own for the normalized
files before anything is measured.

There is nothing else to set. The batch always fits a **cubic B-spline**
continuum with interior knots every 20 nm and 20 clipping iterations, and
that is deliberate rather than a shortcut: a batch is normalized *blind* —
thirty continuum fits go past with nothing overlaid on a template — so the
model that runs has to be the one that copes with a whole queue unattended.
A B-spline follows a wide range through its knots instead of one global
curve, so it does not need an order chosen per spectrum the way a
polynomial does, and a wrong polynomial order is precisely the mistake that
cannot be caught when nothing is being previewed. The single-file
**Normalize raw...** dialog still offers the polynomial, an adjustable
order and a preview against the template, and remains the place to tune one
spectrum by eye.

The raw frames are never touched. Each normalized copy is written as
`<name>_norm.txt` with its own `<name>_normalization.png` continuum check,
and carries the target metadata and the wavelength-frame evidence in its
comment header, so the barycentric machinery keeps working on it. The queue
is then replaced by those copies and the tick clears itself, so a second run
does not normalize what is already normalized. If your spectra are already
normalized, leave the box unticked; that is the default.

Below the normalization box, **Settings for every spectrum** lists what the
run takes from the main window, one line each, the way the Method panels do:
Method (the summary line under **Method...**), Template, Wavelength range,
R, vsini, Target, Barycentric, Ephemeris and Observation time. Nothing is
edited there — change a setting on the main window and reopen **Batch...**.

**The observation time is the one thing not shared.** Each spectrum's own
header (or file name) supplies its epoch, and with it its BJD, its
barycentric correction and its orbital phase — the *Observation time* line
says *from each file's header*. If the **Obs. time** box on the main window
holds a value, the same line adds in orange that it is ignored here: one
typed timestamp used for ten files would give all ten the same BJD, the same
v_bary and the same phase, and the RV curve that came out would be flat and
wrong without looking wrong.

Before the run starts, the queue's wavelength frames are checked together,
because the **Apply barycentric correction** tick is a single switch and the
hint beside it describes the file in the spectrum box — which is not one of
these. What the check finds is reported, and the run is yours to confirm:
a queue that mixes barycentric and topocentric files asks whether to go
ahead as ticked (one setting cannot be right for both unless a header is
wrong, so two batches are usually the answer), and a queue that is entirely
barycentric while the box is ticked asks before adding v_bary a second time
— 20–30 km/s of Earth's motion counted twice. Neither one unticks the box
for you, and neither one stops the batch: a "corrected" verdict can rest on
a pipeline rule or on a Julian date, which is a time stamp and no
statement about wavelengths at all ([section
7](#7-barycentric-frame-what-the-headers-say-and-who-decides)). A queue that mixes the two PolarBase products
(normalized `i` and not-normalized `i_u`) asks before running, because
their wavelength zero points differ by ~0.15 km/s and the curve would carry
that step.

Two method settings are refused for a batch, both for the same reason —
they are about one epoch, not a series:

- **BF with assumptions.** The starting guesses are read off one BF curve.
  Between epochs the components move by tens of km/s, so the same guess
  applied to a whole series pulls every epoch back towards the first one's
  answer. Switch to the automatic component search.
- **TODCOR self-calibration.** It builds its composites from the templates
  and reads no observed spectrum at all, so a list of spectra changes
  nothing about it.

A progress window names the file being measured and has a **Stop** button;
stopping keeps everything measured so far. An epoch that fails is not lost
either — it appears in the results with its error message instead of a
figure, so a broken file is visible rather than silently missing.

**The epochs run in parallel.** Each epoch is measured in a
worker process of its own, with one BLAS thread, by the same `ccf`, `bf` or
`todcor` call; the log still lists the epochs in file order. The first
epoch runs alone and shows how much memory one epoch needs (BF: about
1.5 GB for 50 nm of HARPS at ±300 km/s). After it, as many workers run as
the free memory holds, at most one per physical core; the log gives the
number. The progress window then names the files that are running, and
**Stop** lets them finish. Example: 25 KX Aqr HARPS spectra, BF with three
components and Bayesian errors: about 40 min one at a time, about 10 min
with four workers. The velocities agree with a one-at-a-time run within
the Monte Carlo noise of the Bayesian errors (rms 0.05σ). To measure one
spectrum at a time on the main thread, start the program with the environment variable `RVCALPY_JOBS=1`;
`RVCALPY_JOBS=N` allows at most N workers. When the workers cannot start,
or one of them stops, the rest of the batch runs one spectrum at a time.
The command-line `batch` measures one spectrum at a time.

### Reading a batch: the overview and the detail window

The results open as **one big window, as large as the screen allows** — one
card per spectrum, laid out in as many columns as fit and re-flowed when the
window is resized. Each card carries a small thumbnail of that epoch's fit,
the file name, one line per component with the velocity and its error, the
BJD, the phase, v_bary and a **keep this epoch** tick. The thumbnails are
deliberately small: a card is there to be *recognised*, not read — whether
the peaks are where they should be, whether one epoch looks unlike its
neighbours — and at this size a fifteen-epoch series fits on one screen
without scrolling. The readable copy is one click away.

**Click a card and it opens at ordinary size**: the full report, the
full-resolution figure with the matplotlib navigation bar to zoom and pan
it, and **Previous**/**Next** (or the ← → keys) to walk the series without
going back and forth. The big overview steps aside while you read a single
result and **comes back the moment you close the detail window** — the
Back button, the window's close box or the Esc key — so there is only ever
one window to look at.

The **keep** tick is the choice you carry to the whole series. Untick the
epochs whose fit you do not trust — the card shows enough to judge, the
detail view shows the rest — and the ones left ticked are the ones that
become the series. It is the same tick in both views: changing it in the
detail window moves the card, and the header keeps a running count.
**Keep all** and **Keep none** are in the footer.

- **RV curve...** plots the kept epochs against orbital phase when the
  window has an ephemeris, and against BJD otherwise, in a zoomable window.
  **Hold the mouse over a point** and a small box shows that epoch: the
  file name, BJD_TDB and the UTC date, the phase, every component's RV
  with its error (the one under the cursor marked `>`), v_bary and whether
  it was applied, the continuum S/N over the measured wavelength range
  (e.g. `S/N @ 500 - 550 nm`), the peak significance of the method, and
  α for TODCOR. The S/N is one value per spectrum over the whole range
  measured, not at a single wavelength. The box is only on screen; the
  saved `result_RV_curve.png` has none.
- **Orbit solution...** — right after **RV curve...** — fits the orbit
  of the kept epochs **directly from the batch's RVs** (RV1 and RV2 of
  each kept card, with their errors and BJDs, in the frame the curve is
  in), so no RV table has to be saved and read back first. It needs two
  components. It opens the same **Orbit** box as the main window, with the
  batch's period filled in, in the same basic mode (no swap repair). The
  result opens in the orbit window (Orbit fit / Corner plot / Wilson plot /
  Report tabs, its own **Save...**), and from then on **Save all
  kept...** offers its six files too. Tick or untick an epoch afterwards
  and the orbit no longer matches the kept set: it is then not offered
  until **Orbit solution...** is run again.
- **Save all kept...** first asks what to write, in two boxes of tick
  boxes:

  - **Each spectrum's folder** — the same choices as **Save** (report,
    figure, line-check figure, profile table, rest-frame spectrum), one
    choice for every kept epoch. Untick them all and no spectrum folder
    is made.
  - **Combined files** — one box per file of the whole series:
    **Overview** (`result_batch_overview.png`), **Batch report**
    (`result_batch_report.txt`), **RV curve figure** (`result_RV_curve.png`),
    **RV curve table** (`result_RV_curve.txt`) and, for a BF batch with
    two or more profiles, **BF profiles figure** (`result_BF_profiles.png`),
    and after an **Orbit solution...** fit **Orbit report** (`result_orbit.txt`),
    **Orbit figure** (`result_orbit.png`), **Corner plot (orbit
    posterior)** (`result_orbit_corner.png`), **Wilson plot (orbit)**
    (`result_orbit_wilson.png`), **Posterior table: orbit parameters**
    (`result_orbit_posterior.txt`) and **RV table: total errors and
    residuals (orbit)** (`result_orbit_rv.txt`).
    Only files the run can produce are offered: no curve without a kept
    epoch, no curve figure without a time on one, no orbit file without an
    orbit fitted to the epochs kept now.

  Both boxes open with your last choice (all ticked the first time). Then
  it asks for a second directory — the normalized spectra went into their
  own, this one is for the results; a new folder name is created — and
  writes what was ticked:

  - **one subdirectory per kept epoch, named after the spectrum**, holding
    the files ticked for it: its own `result_*.txt`, its fit figure, its
    line-check figure, its profile table `result_*_profile.txt` and its
    rest-frame spectrum `result_*_shifted*.fits`. The naming is not cosmetic: every single-spectrum run calls its
    files `result_BF.txt` and `result_BF.png` whatever spectrum it measured,
    so ten epochs written side by side into one folder would be one epoch
    written ten times.
  - **`result_batch_report.txt`** — the whole run as one document, and the
    file to read, keep or send on. Its heading names the **star** the run
    measured (every star, with its epoch count, when the queue held more
    than one). It opens with **ALL EPOCHS**, an aligned table carrying one
    line per spectrum — every queued spectrum, so the table is a record of
    the run and not only of its successes. The columns are:

    | column | meaning |
    |---|---|
    | `spectrum` | the file, its middle cut out when it is too long for the column |
    | `BJD_TDB` | mid-exposure barycentric Julian date; `-` means the epoch or the coordinates were missing |
    | `phase` | orbital phase from the ephemeris on the window; `-` when none was given |
    | `RVn` | the velocity as reported — barycentric when the correction was applied, measured otherwise — with its error |
    | `v_bary` | the barycentric correction; the heading of the report says whether it was added to the RVs — the `barycentric` line, and next to it a `wavelength fr.` line with the queue's header verdicts (how many files call themselves already barycentric, topocentric or unknown) and, when v_bary was added to files whose headers say it is already in them, the same warning the run printed |
    | `S/N` | continuum signal-to-noise per pixel of the spectrum |
    | `peak` | significance of the correlation or BF peak (TODCOR: the 2-D peak correlation); below ~4 the velocity is not trustworthy |
    | `status` | kept, dropped or failed |

    The table carries no legend of its own: the columns mean the same thing
    in every run, so they are written down here once instead of into every
    file the program produces. After the table come the settings the run was
    made with (including the normalization, when one ran), every epoch's
    full report in the order measured, the epochs dropped or failed with the
    reason, and the velocity table.
  - **`result_batch_overview.png`** — the gallery itself as one picture:
    every kept epoch's figure at readable size, with its file name above
    it and no caption below. Like every saved figure it is a 600 dpi
    image, each epoch's panel 4 in (2400 px) wide. The figures carry their own BJD, phase and
    velocities — BF and CCF in the legend, TODCOR in the line under the
    figure, barycentric values included when the correction was applied
    — so a caption would only repeat them.
  - **`result_RV_curve.txt`** — the same column layout the command-line
    [`batch`](#batch--a-time-series-into-an-rv-curve) writes, beginning
    with the spectrum (no star column; the batch report's heading names the
    star). It feeds
    [`wilson`](#wilson--mass-ratio-from-the-rv1-vs-rv2-plot) and
    [`orbit`](#orbit--sb2-orbital-elements-the-last-step-of-the-chain)
    directly — plus `result_RV_curve.png`, and in BF mode
    `result_BF_profiles.png`, every epoch's profile stacked by phase.
  - **The RV curve figure is one panel: `RV [km/s]` over `Phase`.**
    The x axis is the orbital phase when an ephemeris was given (from
    −0.25 to 1.25, each epoch drawn once more one cycle to either side),
    and `BJD_TDB` when it was not; the y axis is the velocity, component 1
    in royal blue, component 2 in maroon and a third in green, without error
    bars. Nothing else
    is drawn — v_bary is a column of `result_RV_curve.txt`, where the
    value can be read exactly rather than off a strip of plot. The
    title names the frame, `(barycentric)` or `(measured frame)`, so a
    figure read months later still says which one the curve is in.

Nothing is written until you press one of the Save buttons, exactly as for a
single run. **Save this one...** in the detail window writes just the epoch
on screen, after the same question about which files.

The command-line [`batch`](#batch--a-time-series-into-an-rv-curve) command
is the right tool for an unattended run over a long
series; the GUI batch is for the case where you want to *look* at every fit
before deciding which epochs to keep.

Without `tkinter` or without a display, a **terminal wizard** takes over:
it walks through the target, the files and the settings for **one** spectrum
(a raw one is normalized first: continuum model, B-spline by default, with a
polynomial order asked only for the polynomial, and the clipping
iterations) and runs `ccf` or `bf` on it. It is not a replacement for the whole window —
TODCOR, the Wilson plot and the batch are not in it — so for a series
without a display use the command-line
[`batch`](#batch--a-time-series-into-an-rv-curve), which needs no display at
all.

### Common examples

Command shapes, with placeholder file names. For runnable commands on the
bundled data — with the output they produce — see
[section 2](#2-quick-start).

```bash
# Wilson plot — mass ratio, systemic velocity and which star is the
# primary, from a finished RV series; swapped epochs are repaired
python rv_analysis.py wilson --rv-file summary_rv_values.txt \
       --star TYC_3805-923-1 --dataset x/RED --method ccf

# ... or one column per component, by number (0 = first column) or by
# name, for a table whose columns are called anything at all
python rv_analysis.py wilson --rv-file rv_pairs.txt --rv1-col 3 --rv2-col 1 \
       --err1-col 4 --err2-col 2

# ... and the orbit from the same velocities (its report ends with a basic
# Wilson plot of the same pairs)
python rv_analysis.py orbit --rv-file summary_rv_values.txt \
       --star TYC_3805-923-1 --period 6.1234

# CCF — single star (SB1); weighted line-mask CCF by default
python rv_analysis.py ccf --spectrum spectrum.txt --template template.prf

# CCF — hot star with strong rotation: widen the RV grid; the hydrogen
# lines are already excluded from the mask (use --keep-balmer to undo,
# --mask-depth to change the line-selection threshold,
# --ccf-mode template for the full-spectrum correlation)
python rv_analysis.py ccf --spectrum hotstar.txt --template template.prf \
    --rv-min -400 --rv-max 400 --vsini 150

# BF — binary star (SB2), two components (automatic component search)
python rv_analysis.py bf --spectrum spectrum.txt --template template.prf \
    --components 2

# BF — triple system, starting from your own estimates read off the BF plot
# (run the automatic mode first, inspect result_BF.png, then:)
python rv_analysis.py bf --spectrum spectrum.txt --template template.prf \
    --components 3 --guess 15 -250 0 --guess-amp 0.02 0.002 0.005 \
    --guess-sigma 50 25 10

# TODCOR — SB2 whose peaks have merged; one template for both components.
# Check the templates on noiseless composites FIRST (no spectrum needed):
python rv_analysis.py todcor --simulate --template1 template.prf \
    --sim-sep 10 20 40 --spectrograph NARVAL
python rv_analysis.py todcor --spectrum spectrum.fits --template1 template.prf \
    --spectrograph NARVAL --dv 1

# continuum-normalize a raw spectrum first (cubic B-spline; --fit-method poly
# for the windowed polynomial)
python rv_analysis.py normalize --spectrum raw.fits --output spectrum_norm.txt

# is the barycentric correction already applied in these FITS files?
# (reads every header; advises whether to use --no-bary)
python rv_analysis.py barycheck --spectra 'data/ADP.*.fits'

# raw ESO frames (HARPS.*/FEROS.*.fits)? download their reduced
# ADP counterparts from the ESO archive next to them, then analyze those
python rv_analysis.py fetch-adp --spectra 'data/FEROS.*.fits'

# a whole time series into an RV curve
python rv_analysis.py batch --spectra 'data/*.fits' --normalize \
    --template template.prf --components 2 --object "*" --varastro

# PolarBase ESPaDOnS/NARVAL: the pipeline-normalized .fts products of every
# epoch (never mix them with the *_u ones); already heliocentric, so
# --no-bary, and the helio->barycentric term is added automatically
python rv_analysis.py batch --spectra 'polarbase/*/[0-9]*i.fts' \
    --method ccf --template template_17000K.txt --spectrograph ESPaDOnS \
    --no-bary --wave-min 400 --wave-max 550
```

#### Julian dates: full or reduced

An ephemeris epoch is published both ways — `2459553.00338` and
`59553.00338` are the same instant, the second one reduced by 2 400 000 —
and it is easy to type whichever the source used. **RVCalPy accepts both.**
Every date it works with internally is a full Julian date, so any value
below 1 000 000 is read as the reduced form and 2 400 000 is added to it:

```
--t0 59553.00338     ->  Note: Ephemeris T0 = 59553.00338 is a reduced Julian
                         date; RVCalPy works in full JD and uses
                         2459553.00338 = 59553.00338 + 2400000.
--t0 2459553.00338   ->  used as it stands
```

The promotion is never silent: the note above is printed (and lands in the
run log), and in the GUI the T0 field is rewritten to the full value so what
you see is what is used. The rule holds everywhere a date is entered —
`--t0` on every subcommand, `--obstime`, the terminal wizard, the GUI field,
and the epoch fetched from VarAstro, which publishes it in both forms.

It matters because nothing downstream would catch the mistake. A reduced T0
subtracted from a full BJD gives a phase that is wrong but perfectly finite:
`(BJD - T0)/P mod 1` still returns a number between 0 and 1, the figure
still folds, and only the physics is nonsense. Feeding a short date to the
HJD → BJD conversion is worse still, since the light-travel terms would be
computed for the wrong position of the Earth.

**One form is not recognized: MJD.** A Modified Julian Date is `JD -
2 400 000.5`, half a day away from the reduced JD above, and it falls in
exactly the same range of numbers — `59553.00338` alone cannot say which of
the two it is. RVCalPy always assumes the reduced JD. If your epoch is an
MJD, add the half day yourself and pass the full JD (`MJD + 2400000.5`); the
note printed on every promotion repeats that value so it can be checked at a
glance.

### `ccf` — cross-correlation

| Option | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `--rv-min`, `--rv-max` | RV scan window [km/s]. Omit both for the automatic two-stage search (coarse ±900 km/s at 10 km/s, then refined around the peak) |
| `--rv-step` | Fine RV step [km/s] |
| `--ccf-mode {mask,template}` | `mask` (default): weighted line-mask CCF, hot-star safe. `template`: the full-spectrum cross-covariance |
| `--mask-depth` | Minimum template line depth for a mask entry |
| `--keep-balmer` | Keep the hydrogen Balmer/Paschen lines in the mask (default: excluded) |
| `--no-clean` | Skip the spike rejection and continuum re-flattening done before the correlation (on by default) |
| `--chunks K` | Number of wavelength chunks for the chunk error (default 5; 0 = the width error only) |

#### Cleaning and the RV error

**Cleaning.** Before the correlation, every order of the observed spectrum
goes through the step `todcor` uses: pixels more than 5 MAD above or 10 MAD
below the median are set to the median, and the continuum is re-flattened
with the windowed polynomial, in every CCF mode. On a synthetic 17 000 K
star with 60 cosmics, the full-template and FFT velocities are 3–6 km/s
off without the cleaning and within 0.2 km/s with it.

The CCF adds **one guard** to the `todcor` rule: a pixel between 0 and 1.2
is never a spike. At high S/N the lines, not the noise, set the MAD, and
10 MAD below the median falls inside the deep lines. Without the guard each
of the 16 UVES spectra of the [CCF benchmark](README.md#benchmark) loses
755–2946 line-core pixels and its velocity moves by up to 0.5 km/s, and the
17 000 K synthetic loses its correlation peak. In noisy spectra (S/N ≈ 10)
the MAD limits already lie outside 0–1.2, and the guard changes nothing.
`todcor` keeps its own rule without the guard. `--no-clean` switches the
cleaning off.

**The RV error** is the larger of two errors, the **width error** and the
**chunk error**. The report gives both:

```
RV error            : from the CCF width (Zucker 2003): C = 0.8559, N = 199789 px; profile-fit error 0.0067 km/s; chunks: 0.0514 km/s (K = 5 of 5, q = 1.15, tau = 0.0914 km/s); adopted 0.0514 km/s = max(formal, chunks)
```

The **width error** is the one-dimensional maximum-likelihood error of
Zucker (2003, MNRAS 342, 1291):

```
sigma_v^2 = -[ N * C''(v) / C(v) * C(v)^2 / (1 - C(v)^2) ]^-1
          = (1 - C^2) * sigma^2 / (N * C * A)
```

where C(v) is the correlation coefficient of the spectrum with the template
shifted to v, N the number of spectrum pixels, and A and sigma the
amplitude and width of a Gaussian fitted to C(v) at the measured velocity
(so C'' = −A/sigma²). The width error therefore grows when the S/N falls
(C drops) and when the lines are broad (sigma grows). N counts the pixels
of the file: in a spectrum resampled finer than its detector pixels (s1d
products) neighbouring pixels share their noise, and the width error is
then too small by the square root of the oversampling. C(v) is always
computed with the full template, so the line-mask and FFT velocities get
the error of the same template match.

The **chunk error** measures the RV again in K equal wavelength chunks of
the overlap of spectrum and template (`--chunks`, default 5) and combines
the K velocities with a random-effects model (DerSimonian & Laird 1986;
the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman variance with q ≥ 1, Röver, Knapp &
Friede 2015). In the report, tau is the scatter between the chunks that
their own errors do not explain — template mismatch, continuum, blends
that differ with wavelength — and q the variance factor. A Student t
factor for K − 1 degrees of freedom (1.14 for K = 5) pays for estimating
that scatter from a few chunks. A chunk narrower than 4 nm is not made
(there are fewer chunks then), and with fewer than 3 usable chunks there
is no chunk error. The width error sees only the noise; the chunk error
also sees the template mismatch, so at high S/N it is usually the larger
one.

The **profile-fit error** is the `curve_fit` covariance of the Gaussian
fitted to the CCF. It shows how well a Gaussian describes the profile,
shrinks with every extra velocity bin, and knows nothing of the noise. It
is the formal error only when C(v) has no positive peak.

The adopted error goes into the error columns of `result_CCF.txt` and
`result_RV_curve.txt`, and the `RV error` line into `result_CCF.txt` as a
`#` comment. On HD 105637 (12 Neo-Narval spectra, S/N ≈ 10, 560–627 nm), a
circular orbit fitted with the width errors gives χ²_red = 3.2; with the
profile-fit errors it gives 41.

### `todcor` — two-dimensional correlation (blended SB2s)

When the two peaks of a 1-D CCF or BF are no longer cleanly separated,
the answer is not a better fit to that 1-D profile. TODCOR ([Zucker & Mazeh 1994](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994ApJ...420..806Z/abstract),
ApJ 420, 806; [Mazeh & Zucker 1994](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00984538),
Ap&SS 212, 349; review: [Zucker 2012](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1743921311027852),
IAU Symp. 282, 371) correlates the spectrum against the *combination*
t₁(v₁) + α·t₂(v₂) and maximises over both velocities at once:

$$R(v_1,v_2)=\sqrt{\frac{c_1^2-2c_1c_2c_{12}+c_2^2}{1-c_{12}^2}}$$

from three ordinary 1-D correlations — c₁ = obs×t₁, c₂ = obs×t₂,
c₁₂ = t₁×t₂. Because c₁₂ encodes how much the templates resemble each other
at a given velocity difference, **the blend is modelled rather than fitted
around**. It needs the **spectrum and two templates**, so it cannot be run on
a MIDAS `xcorr` product — only on the spectrum that went into one.

#### One template or two?

TODCOR always needs two template *arrays* — c₁₂ is what models the blend — but
that is **not** the same as needing two different files. For a twin binary you
pass the same template twice, and that is the usual case:

```bash
# twin binary — omit --template2 and --template1 is used for both
rv_analysis.py todcor --spectrum star.fits --template1 5215K.prf --dv 1
```

In the widget, leave **Template 2** (grey hint *empty: same as template 1*) blank.

**What actually happens when Template 2 is empty.** Nothing is skipped and no
component is dropped — `--template1` is simply loaded a second time and used as
template 2, so the fit still solves for two velocities and a light ratio. What
changes is that t₁ and t₂ become bit-identical, and that has four consequences,
all of them handled but all worth knowing:

1. **The surface becomes exactly symmetric** under (v₁, v₂, α) → (v₂, v₁, 1/α).
   The mirror maximum is not merely close in height, it is the same height, so
   **which star gets label 1 is decided by rounding noise.** The run prints a
   notice saying so.
2. **Component 1 is then defined as the brighter star** (α ≤ 1), matching `bf`'s
   C1 when `bf` runs without an ephemeris (with one, `bf` labels by orbital
   phase instead — [section 10](#10-component-labels)). It is a light-ratio
   ordering, not a spectroscopic identification, so near α = 1 the two can
   still trade places from one epoch to the next. A fixed `--alpha` is left
   in the order you asked for.
3. **The v₁ = v₂ diagonal turns singular**, not just uninformative: c₁ = c₂ and
   c₁₂ = 1 exactly there, the optimal-α expression goes 0/0, and an unguarded
   peak search would return that NaN and rail the fit at −`--max-rv`. The ridge
   is excluded from the search, so this is a non-event — but it is why the
   exclusion is not optional.
4. **α is measured against a single spectral type.** If the two stars really do
   differ, α absorbs the mismatch and stops being a clean flux ratio.

**If you do not want that**, give a genuinely different `--template2` — or
`--teff2`, which downloads one (`--teff1` does the same for template 1);
either is enough to leave the twin path. Note
that the path is chosen from the **arrays**, not from the arguments, so passing
the same file twice by hand is the same thing as leaving Template 2 empty.

Reach for two *different* templates only when the two stars really differ in
spectral type. Two near-identical but non-identical templates are the worst
choice: they break the mirror symmetry described below for no physical reason,
which makes the component labels unstable. On HD 105637, a 5240 K + 5215 K pair
(the two templates 95.5 % correlated with each other) put label 1 on the
54 km/s star over the full range and on the 35 km/s star over a 500 Å subset —
same data, same template order.

#### Component labels are a convention, not a measurement

`rv1`/`rv2` mean "the velocity found for `--template1` / `--template2`". **No
sorting is applied**, so they are not comparable to a column that *is* sorted —
a MIDAS `VA`/`VB` pair, for instance, is ordered by velocity (`VA < VB` always),
so a TODCOR result that looks swapped against it is usually the same
measurement with different labels.

The surface also has a **mirror maximum** at (v₂, v₁, 1/α). With one template
for both components it is *exactly* as high — the surface equals its own
transpose to 2×10⁻⁸, and the two α values multiply to 1.0000 — so which star
gets label 1 is decided by rounding noise. (With two different templates it is
merely *almost* as high: on the 5240 K + 5215 K pair, swapping the two template
files reproduced the answer with α inverted, 0.4290 → 2.3312, at identical peak
correlation.) The run says so:

```
Both components use the same template: the surface is exactly symmetric under
(v1, v2, alpha) -> (v2, v1, 1/alpha), so the component labels are a convention,
not a measurement. They are fixed here by light ratio - component 1 is the
brighter star (alpha <= 1), the same rule bf uses for C1/C2.
```

So **identify components by brightness or by orbital phase, never by the
label**. The result file gives the label-independent statement directly:

```
Brighter    : component 1 (RV = 55.60 km/s), by a factor 1.12 in flux
```

#### The v₁ = v₂ ridge is excluded

Two components at the same velocity are indistinguishable from a single star,
so the flux ratio there is not a measurable quantity and the diagonal is never
a legitimate maximum. It is removed from the peak search. With one template
this is also a hard numerical necessity: t₁ and t₂ are then bit-identical, so
c₁ = c₂ and c₁₂ = 1 exactly on the diagonal, the engine's optimal-α expression
becomes 0/0, and an unguarded `argmax` would return that NaN and rail the fit
at −`--max-rv`. `--min-sep` widens the excluded band, for near-identical
templates where the ridge is ill-conditioned rather than exactly singular; if
the reported separation comes back equal to your `--min-sep` (+1 px), the
constraint is what is setting it, not the data.

**Which of BF and TODCOR to believe.** The two are complementary, and the
[saphires](https://saphires.readthedocs.io/en/latest/intro.html) package —
whose BF practices RVCalPy already follows — states the trade cleanly: BFs
"generally … perform better than CCFs and TODCOR at low signal-to-noise",
while for small velocity separations TODCOR is "actually better than a BF";
TODCOR's results "depend entirely on input template accuracy" and are "more
sensitive to mismatches, particularly flux ratio measurements". So: run both,
trust the BF when the exposure is faint and the peaks are clean, trust TODCOR
when the peaks merge, and treat a fitted α far from the expected light ratio
as a statement about the templates rather than about the star.

**Exact vs regular TODCOR.** The correlations are normalized over the
spectral bins that actually overlap at each lag, following Tonry & Davis
(1979) — the same convention saphires uses — rather than over the full array
length. That requires the templates to be longer than the spectrum by twice
the search half-width, so the log-λ grid is built with the templates extended
by *m* pixels at each end (`--max-rv` / `--dv` pixels; 200 by default). Given
equal-length arrays the engine has nothing to slide into the window, warns,
and falls back to building the template–template term c₁₂ from a single 1-D
correlation with global standard deviations — the "regular" TODCOR. Since c₁₂
is exactly the term that models the blend, that approximation is weakest
where TODCOR is used at all. The result line says which ran:

```
Normalization       : exact TODCOR - every correlation normalized over the 58823 overlapping bins (Tonry & Davis 1979 convention, as in saphires)
```

On the HD 105637 Neo-Narval spectrum (components 10 km/s apart) the exact
form moves the pair by 0.08 km/s and tightens the separation from 10.031 to
9.958 km/s.

The engine is [Simchon/TODCOR](https://github.com/Simchon/TODCOR) (MIT),
included unchanged at the end of `rv_analysis.py`.

| Option | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `--spectrum` | Normalized observed spectrum |
| `--template1` | Template for component 1 (required) |
| `--template2` | Template for component 2. **Omit for a twin binary** and `--template1` is used for both |
| `--vsini1`, `--vsini2` | Rotational broadening per template [km/s]. Without `--vsini2`, template 2 takes the `--vsini1` value. `--vsini2 0` gives no rotation to template 2 |
| `--dv` | km/s per pixel of the log-λ grid (default 1.0) |
| `--max-rv` | Half-width of the velocity search (default 200 km/s) |
| `--alpha` | Fix the light ratio instead of fitting it |
| `--min-sep` | Exclude \|v₁ − v₂\| below this [km/s] from the peak search (default 0; the v₁ = v₂ ridge itself is always excluded) |
| `--no-clean` | Skip the spike rejection / continuum re-flattening |
| `--chunks K` | Chunks for the chunk error (default 5; 0 = off). The reported error is the larger of the formal error and the chunk error, as for `ccf` ([Cleaning and the RV error](#cleaning-and-the-rv-error)) |
| `--simulate`, `--sim-sep` | Torres-style self-calibration (see below) |

**The log-λ grid is not optional.** TODCOR measures shifts in *pixels*, so a
pixel must be a constant velocity — Zucker (2012) states it as
n = A·ln λ + B. The command builds that grid itself from whatever sampling the
spectrum and templates arrive on.

**Clean the spectrum or the correlation will measure the wrong thing.** A
correlation is dominated by whatever has the largest amplitude, and in a
pipeline-normalized echelle order that is cosmics, dead pixels and order-edge
roll-off rather than the stellar lines. On the Neo-Narval `st3` orders (flux
running −1 to +10 about a continuum near 0.7) skipping this step drops the
peak correlation from 0.16 to 0.12 and moves the secondary velocity by 20 km/s.
Per-order spike rejection plus continuum re-flattening is therefore on by
default; `--no-clean` exists only to show what it does.

#### Reading α, the light ratio

α is not a setting — it is **fitted**, alongside v₁ and v₂. The model is

```
model = template(shifted to v1)  +  α × template(shifted to v2)
```

so α is the dial for how much light the second component contributes: α = 1
means equally bright, α = 0.5 means the second star gives half as much light.
The command tries every combination of v₁, v₂ and α and keeps the one that
correlates best.

Because the surface is symmetric, α and 1/α are two equally good descriptions
of the *same* star — and they agree on the physical quantity:

```
 reported solution: alpha=0.894  v1=+56 v2=+32  ->  light(+56)/light(+32) = 1.119
   mirror solution: alpha=1.119  v1=+32 v2=+56  ->  light(+56)/light(+32) = 1.119
```

That ratio is what the `Brighter` line reports, and it is the number to quote.

**Check how flat the maximum is before believing α.** It is routinely the
weakest quantity in the output — saphires notes TODCOR is most sensitive to
template mismatch in the flux ratio first. Scanning α with everything else
re-optimised, on the HD 105637 Neo-Narval epoch:

```
  alpha   best v1  best v2    peak R
  0.400     56.0     32.0   0.11492   ##################
  0.600     56.0     32.0   0.11941   #######################
  0.800     56.0     32.0   0.12111   #########################
  0.894     56.0     32.0   0.12126   ##########################  <- top
  1.000     32.0     56.0   0.12110   #########################
  1.119     32.0     56.0   0.12126   ##########################  <- equal top
  1.400     32.0     56.0   0.12065   #########################
  2.000     32.0     56.0   0.11759   #####################
```

The correlation changes by less than 0.5 % between α = 0.7 and α = 1.4, so the
honest statement is "the two stars are roughly equally bright", not
"α = 0.894 ± 0.064". The velocities, by contrast, are pinned to a fraction of a
km/s across that whole range. If you need a real light ratio, fix it from an
eclipse depth with `--alpha` rather than reading it off the fit.

#### Reading the TODCOR figure

`result_TODCOR.png` (and the figure in the RV result window) has three
panels. The numbers below are those of the example spectrum,
`example_TODCOR_NeoNarval_norm.txt` run with `--no-bary`, where TODCOR
reports **v1 = 55.042 km/s, v2 = 33.149 km/s, α = 0.868**, peak
correlation 0.105.

**1. The TODCOR surface (left).** TODCOR correlates the spectrum with the
two-template model t1(v1) + α·t2(v2) for **every pair** (v1, v2) on the grid,
at the best α for that pair. The panel shows that correlation as an image:
the x axis is the primary velocity v1, the y axis the secondary velocity v2,
and the colour is the correlation (colour bar beside it). The result is the
**single highest point** of this surface, marked with a red **+**. What
to look for:

- one clear, compact maximum — a bright island at (55, 33) in the example;
- a vertical bright band through the + (all pairs with the right v1: the
  primary alone already correlates well) and a weaker horizontal band (the
  secondary). Their crossing is where both are right;
- with **one template for both stars** the surface is mirror-symmetric about
  the diagonal v1 = v2, so a second, equal maximum sits at the swapped pair
  (33, 55); which one is called 1 is a convention (see
  [Component labels are a convention, not a measurement](#component-labels-are-a-convention-not-a-measurement)).
  The diagonal itself is excluded from the search
  ([The v₁ = v₂ ridge is excluded](#the-v₁--v₂-ridge-is-excluded)).

**2. "cut at v2 = …" (middle) — this panel measures v1.** It is a
*slice* of the surface: a horizontal line through the + at the fitted
secondary velocity, i.e. v2 **held fixed** at its result and v1 run along the
x axis (labelled *primary velocity*). The title names the fixed value — in the
example **"cut at v2 = 33.15 km/s"** — and the curve shows how the
correlation changes with **v1**, so its peak is at **v1 = 55.04 km/s**, where
the **royal-blue** dotted line stands. Read it as "the primary's correlation
function once the secondary has been put at its right velocity".

**3. "cut at v1 = …" (right) — this panel measures v2.** The other slice: a
vertical line through the + at the fitted primary velocity, v1 held fixed and
v2 run along the x axis (labelled *secondary velocity*). In the example the
title is **"cut at v1 = 55.04 km/s"**, the curve peaks at **v2 = 33.15
km/s**, and the **maroon** dotted line marks it.

So **each title quotes the velocity that is held fixed, and each panel's
peak and dotted line is the other velocity** — the usual "cut at y =
constant" wording, the same as in the TODCOR papers (Zucker et al. 2003, A&A
404, 775, Fig. 2) and in the TODCOR code's own example. The order is not
swapped: the panel titled with v2 measures v1. The dotted lines use the
components' colours everywhere in RVCalPy — v1 (component 1) royal blue,
v2 (component 2) maroon.

**Why the cuts, and not the 1-D CCF.** Each cut is what an ordinary 1-D CCF
would look like *if the other star's velocity were already known*: the other
star has been taken out by the model, so a peak that the 1-D CCF shows only as
a shoulder of a blend stands alone here. Both cuts peak **exactly** on their
dotted lines by construction — they are slices through the maximum — so a
cut whose peak is broad, flat or not clearly above its side lobes means that
velocity is poorly determined (compare the errors in the report). The two
cuts share the surface's correlation scale, and the right-hand one (the cut
along v2) usually runs higher everywhere: there the brighter primary is
already at its right velocity, so the correlation stays high even away from
v2, while in the middle panel only the fainter secondary is in place. Both
reach the same top value, the peak correlation, at the +. No Gaussians are fitted or
drawn: two Gaussians fitted to a blended 1-D CCF are not a measurement at
small separations (Halbwachs et al. 2017, SF2A, arXiv:1710.02301: reliable
only beyond about 6 × (σ₁ + σ₂)), and their centres can sit several km/s
from the true velocities — a synthetic test with broadened lines put the
weaker one 9 km/s off while TODCOR recovered the input.

**The line under the figure** says which frame is plotted. Without the
barycentric correction it reads `plotted = reported: v1 = 55.042, v2 = 33.149
km/s` (and v_bary, computed but not applied). With the correction applied,
every axis is shifted to barycentric velocities (labels end in `(bary.)`,
the surface and its + move together, so the peak stays on its surface) and
the line reads `plotted = reported (barycentric): v1 = 44.229, v2 = 22.337
km/s    v_bary = -10.811    measured: 55.042, 33.149 km/s`. A heliocentric
spectrum (PolarBase) is plotted as measured and the line gives the
helio → bary term.

**The same curves as numbers.** `result_TODCOR_profile.txt` holds, on one
velocity axis (`RV` in the reported frame, `RV_raw` measured): `corr_v1` =
the middle panel (the surface along v1 at the fitted v2), `corr_v2` = the
right panel (along v2 at the fitted v1), and, not drawn, `ccf_1d` (the
ordinary 1-D CCF against template 1) and `ccf_fit` (a two-Gaussian fit to it).

**When the picture says "check the templates".** The stars' lines must be as
broad as the templates': if the cuts' peaks are much wider than the
template's own correlation peak, give the templates a *v* sin *i* (main window
**vsini**; TODCOR settings **vsini of template 2** copies it unless you
change it) and keep the value that gives the highest **Peak correlation** in the report. A template
sharper than the stars biases v2 by several km/s at small separations
(Mazeh et al. 2001, Gliese 644); for the Neo-Narval epoch
NEO_20260312_195303 of TYC 1917-1311-1, a template vsini of 10–15 km/s raised
the peak correlation from 0.106 to 0.120 and moved v2 by −2 to −3 km/s.

#### `--simulate`: check the templates before trusting the RVs

Following Zucker (2012, sect. 3) after Torres et al. (2009), this builds
**noiseless** composite spectra from the two templates at known velocities,
runs TODCOR on them and reports the residual bias — the floor set by the
templates and the grid alone, before any noise:

```bash
rv_analysis.py todcor --simulate --template1 A.prf --template2 B.prf \
                      --sim-sep 10 20 25 40 90
```

On the 5215 K + 5950 K pair the bias is ≤ 0.02 km/s at every separation from
10 to 90 km/s — including the 17–27 km/s range where a two-Gaussian CCF fit is
already unreliable. That is the concrete reason to prefer TODCOR there. A
single 5215 K template used for both components does as well, ≤ 0.01 km/s from
10 to 60 km/s with α recovered to 0.001:

```
 sep_true   v1_rec   v2_rec   bias1   bias2  sep_err   alpha  swap
     10.0     0.00    10.00   +0.00   -0.00    -0.00   0.601     -
     20.0     0.00    20.00   +0.00   -0.00    -0.00   0.601   yes
     60.0     0.00    60.00   +0.00   -0.00    -0.00   0.601     -
```

The **`swap`** column is the mirror solution winning: v₁/v₂ came back exchanged
and α inverted. The velocities printed are re-matched to the input pair, so a
flip is reported as a swap rather than scored as a bias of one whole
separation. With one template for both components it is expected and harmless —
the two maxima are exactly equal. Between epochs of a real system it is what
makes component labels jump, which is the reason to identify components by α or
by orbital phase.

Repeat it with a deliberately **wrong** template and the failure mode is
obvious: broadening the secondary template to vsini = 25 km/s biases the
secondary velocity by **−4.4 to −6.1 km/s at separations of 17–25 km/s**, while
leaving separations ≥ 40 km/s almost untouched, and it drives the fitted α from
0.6 to ~0.99. This is the effect Halbwachs et al. (2017) blame for the positive
secondary shift, and it is the same few-km/s scale as the discrepancies seen on
HD 105637. **A fitted α far from expectation, or one that changes between
epochs of the same system, means the templates are wrong** — the command warns
when α is wildly implausible.

### `normalize` — continuum normalization

`normalize` (and `batch --normalize`) fits the continuum with iterative
asymmetric sigma-clipping, so the fit converges onto the continuum regions.
Two continuum models are available (`--fit-method`), and the **B-spline is
the default everywhere** — `normalize`, `batch --normalize`, the terminal
wizard and the GUI's **Normalize raw...** (which shows the polynomial order
only when Polynomial is chosen); the GUI's batch always uses the B-spline,
for the reason given [there](#batch-a-whole-series-in-one-run):

- **bspline** (default) — a cubic B-spline with interior knots every
  window (default 20 nm; the saphires `cont_norm` model);
- **poly** — a low-order polynomial (`--poly-order`, default 3); segments
  wider than ~1.5× the window are fitted in 50%-overlapping windows
  blended with triangular weights.

Tuning: `--poly-order` (`--fit-method poly` only), `--iterations` (default 20),
`--low-clip` / `--high-clip` (rejection thresholds in σ, asymmetric on
purpose), `--window` (default 20 nm: the B-spline's knot spacing, or for
`poly` the window width, where `0` = a single polynomial over the whole
segment). Pass `--template` to
overlay a synthetic spectrum on the check figure.

`normalize` copies the target metadata (OBJECT, DATE-OBS, RA, Dec) from the
raw FITS header — or from `--object/--ra/--dec/--obstime` — into the comment
header of the output `*_norm.txt`, so a later `ccf`/`bf` run reads the
coordinates straight from the normalized file and can compute the
barycentric correction without re-entering them. The barycentric-frame
evidence (INSTRUME, SPECSYS or a BARYFRAME verdict card, BERV) is copied
too, so `barycheck` and the automatic check in `batch` keep working on
the normalized ASCII file. For a PolarBase spectrum the metadata comes from
its `.meta` file when the spectrum has no header, and the product it came
from is kept as `PBPRODUCT = normalized / not-normalized`. Re-normalizing an
`i_u` file does not give it the zero point of the pipeline-normalized `i`.

### `wilson` — mass ratio from the RV1-vs-RV2 plot

[Wilson 1941](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1941ApJ....93...29W),
ApJ 93, 29, *On the Determination of Mass Ratios of Spectroscopic Binaries*.
Both components orbit their common centre of mass, so momentum ties them at
**every** epoch:

```
    M₁ (V₁ − γ) = −M₂ (V₂ − γ)
```

Rearranged, that is a straight line in the (V₂, V₁) plane:

```
    V₁ = γ (1 − b) + b·V₂ ,        b = −M₂/M₁ = −K₁/K₂
```

So plotting one component's velocity against the other's gives the **mass
ratio from the slope alone**. No period, no eccentricity, no epoch of
conjunction — no orbit at all. Setting V₁ = V₂ collapses the relation to
(M₁ + M₂)(V − γ) = 0, so **the line crosses the V₁ = V₂ diagonal exactly at
the systemic velocity**. Both statements hold for any eccentricity and any
orientation, which is what makes the method work on a handful of epochs that
could never define an orbit.

```bash
python rv_analysis.py wilson --rv-file summary_rv_values.txt \
       --star TYC_3805-923-1 --dataset x/RED --method ccf
```

Three things come out of it.

**1. Which star is the primary.** |b| = K₁/K₂ = M₂/M₁, and the component with
the **smaller velocity amplitude is the more massive** one. So the plot
decides primary vs secondary on its own — the question §10 above leaves open:

```
Slope             : -0.27816 +- 0.08151   (= -K1/K2 = -M2/M1)
Systemic velocity : 39.14570 +- 5.79171 km/s
Mass ratio        : q = M_sec/M_pri = 0.27816
PRIMARY           : component 1
```

**What the slope should look like.** The slope is a mass ratio wearing a minus
sign, so it is not free to be anything:

| Slope b | Means | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **b < 0** | the two stars move in opposite directions | the only physical case |
| **b = −1** | K₁ = K₂, equal masses (a twin) | normal; reported as primary = 1, but with equal masses that label is arbitrary |
| **−1 < b < 0** | component 1 moves less, so it is the heavier one | normal; q = −b, primary = 1 |
| **b < −1** | component 2 moves less, so it is the heavier one | normal; q = −1/b, primary = 2 |
| **b ≥ 0** | both stars moving the same way | impossible for a bound pair — **warning** |
| **−0.1 < b < 0, or b < −10** | one star barely moves next to the other (q < 0.1) | **warning**: probably not a plain SB2 |

**Which way round the plot is drawn.** By default V₁ is across and V₂ up.
The line is fitted by orthogonal distance, so it is the same line whichever
axis is which, but drawn this way its slope is **1/b = −M₁/M₂**, and that
is the number in the legend: `slope = -1.157 ± 0.000` for LL Aqr, where the
report's b is −0.86453 and the panel title reads `M₂/M₁ = 0.8645`. The
table above, the report and q all use b; **read the legend as the
reciprocal of the title, not as a second measurement.** The two agree only
for a near-twin pair: KX Aqr (q = 0.9859) has a plotted slope of −1.0143,
LL Aqr (q = 0.8645) −1.1567, and TYC 3805-923-1 (q = 0.5839) −1.7125.
`wilson --swap-axes` (the GUI's **Swap axes: V₁ ↔ V₂** button) draws V₂
across and V₁ up; the legend then shows b itself, −M₂/M₁ = −q.

**How each number is computed.** Take a table of TYC 3429-458-1 with
4 epochs, two SOPHIE and two Neo-Narval, errors in `sigma_RV1`/`sigma_RV2`,
swaps not repaired (the GUI default):

1. **The line** V₁ = a + b·V₂ is fitted with the errors of *both*
   velocities (orthogonal distance regression). For a straight line that
   minimises

   ```
       χ² = Σ (V₁ − a − b·V₂)² / (σ₁² + b²·σ₂²)
   ```

   → b = −0.97442 ± 0.01005, a = 1.32845 ± 0.23464 km/s. The errors are the
   fit's covariance scaled by χ²/(n − 2). A precise epoch weighs more: the
   SOPHIE pairs (σ ≈ 0.07 km/s) count about a hundred times more than the
   Neo-Narval ones (σ ≈ 0.6–1.0 km/s), which is why the line passes almost
   exactly through them.
2. **γ** = a / (1 − b) = 1.32845 / 1.97442 = **0.67283 km/s** — where the
   line meets V₁ = V₂. Its error is propagated from a and b with their
   covariance.
3. **q** = |b| = **0.97442** (1/|b| when |b| > 1), and component 1 is the
   primary because |b| < 1.
4. **Residual** of each epoch = its perpendicular distance from the line,
   r = (b·V₂ − V₁ + a) / √(1 + b²) → −0.022, +0.017, +0.632, −2.320 km/s.
   Perpendicular, not vertical, because both axes are measured velocities.
5. **rms** = √(mean r²) = **1.2022 km/s**. Every residual is squared, so
   the worst epoch dominates it.
6. **Robust σ** = 1.4826 × median|r − median r| = 1.4826 × 0.3268 =
   **0.4845 km/s** — the median absolute deviation, scaled to equal σ for
   Gaussian noise. One bad epoch hardly moves it, so an rms well above the
   robust σ means one or two epochs do not fit. The robust σ is also the
   scale of the swap test (`--nsigma` of it) and of the grey bands in the
   figure's residual panel (±1 and ±`--nsigma` robust σ).

With the swap repair on (the command-line default) row 4 is exchanged and
everything above is recomputed: b = −0.97088, rms 0.5112, robust σ
0.4018 km/s. Read without its error columns (equal weights) the same table
gives b = −1.00369, rms 0.9580, robust σ 1.0409 km/s.

Two consequences worth keeping in mind. First, **the reported `q` is always
between 0 and 1**: b = −0.5 and b = −2.0 give the same q = 0.5 and differ only
in which component is called the primary. So it is the *distance of the slope
from −1* that measures how unequal the masses are, and *which side of −1* it
falls on that names the primary — the size of the slope on its own says
neither. Second, **−1 is the boundary, not a target**: at exactly −1 the masses
are equal and the label is a convention, the same convention discussed in §10.

In practice most double-lined systems land somewhere between about **−1 and
−0.2** (q ≈ 0.2 to 1). The reason is not the method but the spectra: a star
much lighter than its companion is also much fainter, and lines you cannot see
give no second velocity, so the very small mass ratios rarely appear in an SB2
to begin with. That is why q < 0.1 is treated as a red flag rather than as an
interesting result — see point 3.

Read the quoted error with the value. On few epochs the line is formally
determined but soft: below 5 epochs the run says so, and a slope whose ±
covers a large fraction of itself (the −0.278 ± 0.082 above is nearly a third)
is an order-of-magnitude statement about q, not a measurement of it.

**2. Mis-identified pairs, found and repaired.** An epoch whose two components
were swapped by the fitting code is the same point **reflected across the
V₁ = V₂ diagonal**, so it sits far off the line and stands out at a glance,
while its mirror image falls straight back onto it. That is both how the swap
is recognised and how it is undone. Repaired epochs are listed as `SWAPPED`
and drawn on the figure with a dashed line back to where they used to be; pass
`--no-fix-swaps` to flag them without touching the values. An epoch is
repaired only when the swap is **decidable**: it lies more than `--nsigma`
robust sigma from the line (default 3), its mirror image lies within
`--nsigma` of it and three times closer, **and** the swap moves the point
across the line by at least `--nsigma` sigma. (`orbit` has options of the
same names, but for its own swap check against the orbit — it does not use
the Wilson plot.)

When anything was repaired, the result also gives the fit **before the
repair** — the velocities exactly as measured, on a line of their own (what
`--no-fix-swaps` would return) — so the effect of the repair can be read off:

```
Scatter           : rms 1.8538 km/s, robust sigma 1.6002 km/s   (after repair)
Before repair     : rms 2.1789 km/s, robust sigma 2.2918 km/s, slope -1.04591, q 0.95611
```

The residual panel of the figure carries both pairs of numbers in its title
(*before repair* on the first line, *after repair* on the second) and draws
the before-repair residuals as grey crosses beside the repaired ones.

This is not ordinary outlier rejection, and it cannot be done with ordinary
robust regression: a swap is a *structured* displacement, and with two epochs
of nine swapped, 42 % of the pairwise slopes a Theil–Sen fit medians over are
contaminated — past its breakdown point. The fit therefore treats every
point's orientation as unknown from the start, drawing candidate lines through
two epochs in either orientation and scoring each by the median distance over
all epochs taken in whichever orientation is closer. That criterion a swap
cannot change, and it tolerates half the set being wrong. Only then is the
line refined by orthogonal distance regression with the errors on **both**
axes.

**Epochs whose swap cannot be tested are reported as `NOT-TESTABLE`, not
repaired.** A swap reflects the point in V₁ = V₂, but only part of that jump
crosses the line: it moves the point's distance from the line by

```
    |1 + b| · |V₁ − V₂| / √(1 + b²)          (b = the slope)
```

Where that is less than `--nsigma` sigma, which component is which is not a
measurable thing, and the epoch is left in the order it came in rather than
"repaired" with a swap that means nothing. That happens in two cases:

- **near conjunction** (V₁ ≈ V₂), for any mass ratio;
- **at every epoch when q is close to 1** (slope near −1). The equal-mass
  line V₁ + V₂ = 2γ is its own mirror image, so a swapped pair lands on it
  again. A warning then says how many epochs could not be tested. In such a
  system a swap and a velocity offset of a few km/s of that epoch shift the
  point identically, so the Wilson plot cannot tell them apart even where
  the formula above allows a test. **For near-twins, identify the
  components by orbital phase**: `orbit` re-tests every epoch against the
  fitted orbit, where a swap changes each velocity by the whole |V₁ − V₂|
  (see [`orbit`](#orbit--sb2-orbital-elements-the-last-step-of-the-chain)).

On the figure they look like every other epoch: all the epochs that
entered the fit are drawn in one style, so the not-testable ones are only named in the report and in the tooltip that
the GUI shows over a point. They are part of the scatter either way.

**3. Whether it is a binary at all.** A **positive slope** is impossible for a
bound pair — the components must move in opposite directions about the centre
of mass — so it is reported as a hard warning that the two velocity sets are
not the two components of one system. An **extreme mass ratio** earns a
warning of its own: Kovalev, Chen & Han
([2023](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526.1830K), MNRAS 526,
1830) traced exactly such "suspicious mass ratios from the Wilson method" to
triples and to a field star aligned by chance with an SB1. A component whose
velocity barely moves is flagged too, since the slope is then unconstrained.

**The error columns are found by name too**, on the command line and in
the GUI dialog alike. When the two RV columns are named, their errors are
taken from the first of these that the table has:
`ccf_rv1` → `ccf_err1` (this program's own), `RV1[km/s]` → `RV1_err`,
the **`sigma_` form** (`RV1` → `sigma_RV1`), plus `sig_`, `e_`, `err_`, `_error`, `_sigma`. Pick
them by hand with `--err1-col`/`--err2-col` (the GUI's *error* drop-downs)
when a table spells them otherwise.

**This matters more than it looks.** A table read with no errors is fitted
unweighted, and every number moves: TYC 3805-923-1 read without its
`sigma_RV*` columns gives slope −0.247, rms 16.8 km/s, robust σ 25.3 km/s,
and with them −0.584, rms 31.5 km/s, robust σ 14.7 km/s. The per-epoch rows
of `<star>_mass_ratio.txt` show `nan` in the error columns when that happened —
check them before comparing two runs.

**Choosing the two columns.** There are four ways, tried in this order.

`--rv1-col` and `--rv2-col` point at one column per component and override
everything else. A column is given as a **number counted from 0** — `0` is the
first column of the table, so the number is the column's index — or by name
when the file has a header:

```bash
python rv_analysis.py wilson --rv-file anything.txt --rv1-col 3 --rv2-col 1
python rv_analysis.py wilson --rv-file summary_rv_values.txt \
       --rv1-col ccf_rv1 --rv2-col bf_rv2
```

The number is what really counts, and it is what the GUI dropdowns store, so
this works on **any** table: one whose columns are called `V_A`/`V_B` or
nothing at all, one that lists the secondary first, one carrying six velocity
columns of which two are wanted. `--err1-col` / `--err2-col` point at the two
error columns the same way. Left out, the error column is looked up from the
RV column's name, using the spellings listed
[above](#wilson--mass-ratio-from-the-rv1-vs-rv2-plot) (`ccf_err1`,
`RV1_err`, `sigma_RV1`, …); when the table has none of them the errors are
NaN and the line is fitted unweighted.
The columns actually used are echoed at the top of the result text. The
figure's axes carry the plain component labels (V₁, V₂ in km/s), so the
result text is where you check which columns a plot was made from.

Naming the columns is also how to use a table whose two methods label the
components differently — the same epoch can appear as `ccf_rv1 = 103.2 /
ccf_rv2 = 32.2` and `bf_rv1 = 32.3 / bf_rv2 = 103.1`.

`--method ccf` (or `bf`) is the shorthand for the summary layout this program
writes: it takes `<method>_rv1`, `_err1`, `_rv2`, `_err2` by name.

An **RV curve** — the `result_RV_curve.txt` that `batch` and the GUI batch
write — is recognised by its own column names and needs neither:
`RV1[km/s]`/`RV2[km/s]` with their `_err` columns, or, in a curve written with
the barycentric correction applied, `RV1_bary[km/s]`/`RV2_bary[km/s]` — the
barycentric pair, not the `_raw` one, because the line wants every epoch on
one scale. The run says which it took:

```
result_RV_curve.txt: RV curve layout - V1 = RV1[km/s], V2 = RV2[km/s]
```

A curve with only one component is refused: there is no second star to put on
the other axis.

With none of these, a table that has no such header is read as plain
`rv1 [err1] rv2 [err2]` columns.

**One line belongs to one binary, on one velocity scale.** Two filters matter:

- `--star` — a summary holding several systems must be filtered, or the fit
  mixes unrelated stars. Rows from more than one star raise a warning. It
  reads the table's `star` column and matches on letters and digits only —
  so `--star "LL Aqr"` finds the `LL_Aqr` written in the file, and
  `HD_105637` finds `HD 105637`. Only `summary_rv_values.txt` has that
  column; the result tables of this program do not, so give them without
  `--star`, one star per file.
- `--dataset` — restricts to one `# ==== <name> ====` section. This is not
  cosmetic: if one instrument's RVs are barycentric and another's are in the
  measured frame, part of the points slide along the line and the slope is
  corrupted. Mixing datasets raises a warning; `--add-vbary` adds the table's
  `v_bary` column to both components to bring measured-frame rows onto the
  barycentric scale (do **not** use it on rows that are already barycentric,
  such as a SOPHIE `s1d`). In an RV curve it adds the `v_bary[km/s]` column;
  a curve written with the correction applied has none to add — its
  `RV*_bary` columns already contain it — so `--add-vbary` is refused there.

Input is a summary table or an RV curve this program wrote, a plain ASCII
table of `rv1 [err1] rv2 [err2]` per line, or values straight on the command
line:

```bash
python rv_analysis.py wilson --rv1 103.3 72.1 37.1 --rv2 31.8 63.7 -34.6
```

(`--err1` / `--err2` give their errors, one per value.)

Outputs are named after the star: **`<star>_mass_ratio.txt`** and
**`<star>_mass_ratio.png`** (`KX_Aqr_mass_ratio.txt`,
`TYC_3805-923-1_mass_ratio.png`). The star is taken from `--star`, else
from the table's star column when it holds one star, else from the
`star(s)` line of a `result_batch_report.txt` beside the table (an RV curve
names no star itself, its batch report does), else from the table's file
name without its generic parts (`summary_TYC_3805-923-1.txt` →
`TYC_3805-923-1`, `KX_Aqr_RV_curve.txt` → `KX_Aqr`); with none of these the
files are `mass_ratio.*`. `--output` / `--plot` still choose the names
outright, and the GUI's Save uses the same names. The plot is on the
left (V₂ up, V₁ across) with the fitted line, the V₁ = V₂ diagonal and γ marked at their
crossing, the residuals about the line on the right. In the GUI the same
thing sits behind the **Wilson plot...** button and opens in the RV result
window. There, move the mouse over a
point — on either panel — and a box shows that epoch's spectrum name, its
orbital phase (when the table has a `phase` column, as `result_RV_curve.txt`
does), RV1 and RV2 with their errors, its distance from the line and whether
it was repaired; over a grey cross or a *before repair* square it shows the
values before the repair. Choosing an RV curve in the dialog selects its
`RV1[km/s]`/`RV2[km/s]` columns (or the `_bary` pair) by itself.

**Leaving a point out by hand.** In the GUI's Wilson plot a **left click
on a point** — on the plot or in the residual panel — leaves that epoch
out of the fit: the line, q, γ, the scatter and the report are made again
at once, the report gains an `Excluded by hand : <epochs>` line under
`Epochs used`, and the point stays on the plot as a **grey ✕** (*excluded
(click to restore)*). Click the ✕ to take the epoch back. **Save...**
writes the result as it is on screen, exclusions included; the table
itself is not changed. At least 3 epochs must stay in. Clicks are ignored
while the toolbar's pan or zoom tool is on. Exclude as many epochs as you
like, one click each: every ✕ stays on the plot (the axes always span the
excluded points too), and a zoomed view is kept from one click to the
next, so a crowded part of the line can be worked through close up. The
toolbar's history survives the clicks as well: **Back** and **Forward**
step through the zooms made before and after them, and **Home** goes back
to the full view. (A click on a point does nothing while the toolbar's
**Zoom** or **Pan** tool is on — switch it off to exclude.) For LL Aqr, leaving out the
blended epoch at conjunction (ADP.2014-09-24T09:43:51.650) brings the rms
about the line from 0.334 to 0.059 km/s, with q 0.8651 → 0.8653. Only the
main window's **Wilson plot...** has this; the orbit window's Wilson tab
is the basic one.

**Where this sits in the analysis.** The Wilson plot is a checkpoint beside
the orbit: it reads the same velocities and needs no period at all.

```
   spectra -> TODCOR/BF -> RV1, RV2 -> orbit
                              |
                              +-----> WILSON PLOT -> q, γ
```

Looking at the velocities this way before an orbit is fitted is common
practice: MESS (Nachmani, Faigler & Mazeh
[2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05610), MNRAS 546) and Kovalev & Straumit
([2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stab3365), MNRAS 510, 1515) take the
mass ratio and γ from the Wilson relation before, or instead of, an orbit
(see [The usual workflow](#the-usual-workflow)).

Note the **convention** when comparing with those papers: here q = M₂/M₁ =
K₁/K₂ ≤ 1, with the primary the more massive star. Some authors quote the
reciprocal, so check which amplitude is on top before comparing numbers.

`orbit` then takes the same velocities on its own — it does not use the
Wilson plot, so the Wilson q and the orbit's K₁/K₂ are two independent
measurements of the mass ratio, and their agreement is a check worth making.
`orbit` makes that check for you: its report ends with a basic Wilson plot
of the same pairs (no swap repair of its own), drawn in
`result_orbit_wilson.png` — see
[`orbit`](#orbit--sb2-orbital-elements-the-last-step-of-the-chain).

### `orbit` — SB2 orbital elements, the last step of the chain

```
   spectra -> TODCOR/BF -> RV1, RV2 -> ORBIT
```

The period, the eccentricity and the masses need the velocities folded on
time; `orbit` does that. It stands on its own — the Wilson plot is a separate
tool (`wilson`, the main window's **Wilson plot...**), not a step of the
orbit. Swapped epochs (RV1 and RV2 exchanged) are found against the orbit
itself, where a swap changes each velocity by the whole |V₁ − V₂|, so it is
decidable for any mass ratio, near-twins included:

1. a first, **swap-blind** fit counts every epoch in whichever order fits
   better, so even a quarter of the epochs swapped cannot drag it off; its
   two curves are named after the table's own components;
2. each epoch is then swapped where it is off that orbit by more than
   `--nsigma` robust sigma (default 3) as it stands and three times closer
   exchanged — a blended epoch at conjunction fits neither order well, and
   the closer one is still the right identification;
3. ordinary fits follow, the check repeated until nothing changes.

`--no-fix-swaps` fits the velocities in the order the table gives them —
what the GUI's **Orbit...** always does (its basic mode). On synthetic SB2s the
check found every swapped epoch with no false ones — at q = 0.97–1.00 with
2–5 km/s scatter, and at q = 0.6 with 6 of 25 epochs swapped.

```bash
python rv_analysis.py orbit --rv-file summary_rv_values.txt \
       --star TYC_3805-923-1 --period 6.1234
```

It reads its velocities exactly as `wilson` does, so `--method`, or
`--rv1-col`/`--rv2-col` (with `--err1-col`/`--err2-col`) for one column per
component by number or by name, apply here too.

Both components are fitted **jointly**, sharing P, T₀, e, ω and γ, each
keeping its own amplitude. Circular by default — the model Kovalev & Straumit
([2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stab3365), MNRAS 510, 1515) used for the
M11 binaries, two sinusoids sharing P, t₀ and γ — with `--eccentric` freeing e
and ω for a full Keplerian.

| Warning | Meaning, and what to do |
|---|---|
| `chain shorter than 50 autocorrelation times` | The percentiles are not yet stable: raise `--mcmc-steps` |
| `posterior at the prior edge: P, e` | The posterior of that parameter reaches the edge of its prior box, so its error is set partly by the prior. Real at a physical limit (e consistent with 0; P constrained worse than the ±0.1 % window of `--period`); otherwise raise `--prior-scale` |
| `period searched, not given` | No `--period`: the period came from a grid search, and a handful of epochs often admits several aliases. Check it against a light curve before trusting the masses |
| `emcee not installed - least-squares errors` | The posterior was skipped; the table is the least-squares one |
| `corner not installed - no corner plot` | `pip install corner` |
| `pull rms 1.83 of star 2 for near conjunction (16 epochs) is not 0.95 (p ...)` | The total errors do not describe that part of the data (p < 0.001). Near a conjunction: blends or eclipses — use `--exclude-conjunctions`. Small against large errors: the model error is not additive here — compare `--model-error scale` |
| `pulls of star 2 not Gaussian (KS p ...)` | Outlying epochs: look at them (a swap, a blend, a bad spectrum), or use `--robust` |
| `the RV3 error term \|beta3\| x err3 = ... is not small ...` | With `--third-star`: the RV3 errors are more than 0.2 × the RV errors, and they are not propagated. Measure RV3 better, or treat the errors with care |
| `beta3 > 0 (2 sigma) ...` | γ rises with RV3. A third body pulls the binary the other way (β₃ = −M₃/(M₁+M₂) < 0); a positive β₃ points to a zero point that moves all three stars together, or to wrong RV3 labels |

With `--no-mcmc` the section is headed `-- Orbit: least squares --`, the
column `value` is the least-squares best fit and -err = +err its error from
the covariance matrix; q, M sin³i and a sin i then have no error at all.
With a model error it is headed `-- Orbit: posterior maximum
(Laplace) --`: the values are the posterior maximum, the errors the Laplace
(Hessian) errors, including those of s1, s2 and rho.

Minimum masses and the projected separation follow from

```
    M sin³i = P (K₁+K₂)² K_other (1−e²)^{3/2} / (2πG)
    a sin i = (K₁+K₂) P √(1−e²) / (2π)
```

computed from astropy's constants rather than a copied coefficient. **These
are minimum masses**: radial velocities alone never give the inclination, so
true masses need an eclipsing light curve or astrometry.

**The period is the weak point.** With `--period` (or `--varastro` to pull the
ephemeris from var.astro.cz) it may move only within ±0.1 % of that value.
Without one, a grid of trial periods is scanned and the best χ² kept — but a
handful of epochs rarely defines a period uniquely, hence the warning above.
`--period-range MIN MAX` bounds the search. Prefer a period from a light curve
whenever there is one.

#### How the errors are found (emcee)

**Every number in the orbit table is from the Bayesian posterior.** A
least-squares fit still runs first, but only as the starting point the method
needs — its values are not reported:

1. **Least squares** — finds the best fit and the error of each parameter
   from the curvature of χ² at its minimum. These errors assume every
   parameter's error is symmetric and Gaussian, which few epochs, a small e or
   a poorly covered phase range break; that is why they are not the answer.
2. **Priors** — uniform: P inside the ±0.1 % window when `--period` was given (a
   searched period: ±`--prior-scale` × its least-squares error),
   |T₀ − T₀,LSQ| < P/2, e and ω through √e cos ω, √e sin ω in e < 0.95,
   K from 0 to 2 × the peak-to-peak velocity + 50 km/s, γ over the
   velocity range widened by itself, data-set offsets ±50 km/s,
   γ₂ − γ₁ ±5 km/s, the model error s1, s2 from 0 to 5 × the
   larger of the residual rms and the median error of that star, rho from
   −1 to 1. Flat priors do not pull the result towards the least-squares
   value; they only limit where the sampler may go.
3. **Likelihood** — Gaussian. Each spectrum has the covariance
   diag(err₁², err₂²) + S of its two velocities, with the model error S
   sampled with the orbit (next section); with `--model-error scale` every
   RV error is multiplied by the `RV error scale` instead.
4. **Sampling** — [emcee](https://emcee.readthedocs.io/en/stable/)
   (Foreman-Mackey et al. [2013](https://doi.org/10.1086/670067), PASP 125,
   306): 64 walkers start in a small ball around the least-squares solution
   and take 5000 steps each; every step proposes a new orbit and keeps it
   with a probability set by its likelihood. The random seed is fixed, so a
   re-run gives the same numbers.
5. **Result** — for every parameter, and for q, M sin³i and a sin i computed
   from every kept model: the **median is the value**, and the distances to
   the **16th and 84th percentiles are the −/+ errors**.

For a well-covered orbit the posterior agrees with least squares — LL Aqr
above changes only in the last digit. A synthetic test of 60 circular and 60
eccentric orbits gave the 16th–84th interval containing the true value 57–70 %
of the time, as it should. The posterior differs where least squares cannot be
trusted: asymmetric errors on T₀ and ω for a nearly circular orbit, errors on
the masses with few epochs, a parameter running into a physical limit.

Outputs are `result_orbit.txt` (the report), `result_orbit.png` (both
components folded on the period from phase −0.25 to 1.25 — royal blue
and maroon dots, the model curves of the posterior medians in light steel
blue and light coral, γ
dashed — and the residuals of the fit, observed minus model velocity, in
a panel straight underneath, 1.5 : 1; the upper panel is always titled
**Solution Curve**, with the elements in the report, and
the figure title above it is left blank),
`result_orbit_corner.png` (the corner plot), `result_orbit_wilson.png`
(the basic Wilson plot of the same pairs: V₂ against V₁ with the fitted
line and γ, and the residuals about the line),
`result_orbit_posterior.txt` (the posterior table) and
`result_orbit_rv.txt` (every epoch with its total error and O − C). The
error bars of `result_orbit.png` are the total errors, with the
measurement errors as thick bars inside them in the residual panel, and
the epochs left out near a conjunction are open symbols. The report ends with a
`-- Wilson plot --` block: slope and γ with their errors, q, the rms and
robust sigma about the line, and the primary. The Wilson numbers are
computed on the pairs in the order the orbit used them (after its swap
check) and never feed back into the fit; a difference between the two q
values is the independent check described under
[`wilson`](#wilson--mass-ratio-from-the-rv1-vs-rv2-plot). The command line
writes all six; the GUI's orbit window and the batch's **Save all
kept...** ask which. The Wilson plot uses the epochs of the fit only, with
the total errors.

#### The model error and the total RV error

**Why.** The RV error of each epoch — the Bayesian BF error, the CCF and
TODCOR errors — is the error of the *measurement*. It
takes the template, the profile shape and the continuum as exact and the
star as the same at every epoch, and it counts the photon noise only. Real
velocities also carry a **model error**: template mismatch, a profile
shape that is neither Gaussian nor rotational, spots, a blend with the
companion, an instrument zero point that changes from night to night. It
does not get smaller at a higher S/N, and one spectrum cannot show it; the
scatter about the orbit can. 
KX Aqr: the residuals scatter 0.6–1.0 km/s on every spectrograph,
where the errors say 0.45–0.6 km/s, while two spectra of one night agree
within their errors (pull rms 0.8). The measurement error is right; the
rest changes from night to night.

**What the orbit does.** Bayesian calibration treats a model error as a
random quantity with parameters of its own, estimates them with the other
parameters and integrates them out (Kennedy & O'Hagan 2001; the
prediction-error model of Beck & Katafygiotis 1998 and Papadimitriou &
Papadimitriou 2014; the embedded model error of Sargsyan, Najm & Ghanem
2015; for the approximation error of an emulator, Grandón & Sellentin
2022, where the data covariance C becomes C + Σ). The orbit does the
same:

```
v_ik = model_k(t_i) + eps_ik + delta_ik
eps_ik  ~ N(0, err_ik^2)        measurement: the error in the RV table
delta_i ~ N(0, S)               model error of spectrum i, both stars

        | s1^2        rho s1 s2 |
    S = |                       |
        | rho s1 s2   s2^2      |
```

s1 and s2 are the size of the model error of each star, rho the part the
two stars of one spectrum share (an instrument zero point gives rho > 0,
two blended profiles rho < 0). They are parameters of the posterior like
K₁ and K₂ — uniform priors, sampled with the orbit — so the orbit errors
include their uncertainty: no number is put in by hand, and none is fixed
after a first fit (Srinivasan et al. 2012 show how an input taken as exact
in Bayes' rule hides uncertainty). The **total error** of an epoch is

```
err_tot = sqrt(err^2 + s^2)
```

the measurement error and the model error added as the variances of two
independent errors add. These are the errors for an RV table or an RV
curve: `result_orbit_rv.txt`.

| `--model-error` | Covariance of one spectrum | Use |
|---|---|---|
| `additive` (default) | diag(err²) + S | a model error independent from spectrum to spectrum |
| `night` | the same, with one δ for all spectra of a night (less than 0.5 d apart) | a model error that changes only from night to night; more conservative errors |
| `scale` | f² diag(err²), f = √χ²_r of the least-squares fit | every error scaled by one factor (`RV error scale`) |
| `none` | diag(err²) | the errors as given |

**Which rule fits the data.** The `error models` line compares the four
with the Bayesian information criterion, BIC = −2 ln L_max + k ln N
(Schwarz 1978) — the large-sample form of the Bayesian evidence that
Papadimitriou & Papadimitriou (2014) use to choose a prediction-error
model. The best rule has 0; a difference of 2–6 is positive, 6–10 strong
and above 10 very strong evidence against a rule (Kass & Raftery 1995).
`none` far behind means the errors of the table are too small. `additive`
and `scale` close together means the data cannot separate them — mostly
when all epochs have about the same error — and then their K errors are
the same too (KX Aqr: K₂ ± 0.15 km/s either way). `additive` is the
default because the photon-noise error is not too small by a factor
(section 9: the synthetic tests and same-night pairs agree with it), and
because the model error does not shrink when the S/N grows.

**The checks** (`-- RV errors --`). With calibrated errors the pulls
(residual / total error) are Gaussian with rms √((N − p)/N), for N
velocities and p free parameters (the heading gives the number: LL Aqr
0.84, KX Aqr 0.95), in every part of the data. Grandón & Sellentin (2022)
make the same two checks before they trust an error model: Gaussian
(skewness, excess kurtosis, Kolmogorov–Smirnov p), and the same everywhere
(their "sub-volumes"): per data set (`-- Residuals --`), near a
conjunction and at quadrature, for small and for large errors. If the
large-error epochs scatter less than the small-error ones, the model error
is additive; the other way round, it scales with the error. A part that
disagrees (p < 0.001) gives a `WARNING:` line. The columns `data`, `model`,
`total` and `orbit` split the predictive error as Sargsyan et al. (2018)
do: measurement, model error, their sum, and the orbit's own uncertainty
at the epochs.

**Eclipses.** Near a conjunction of an eclipsing binary the model of two
independent profiles does not hold: the profiles touch, and the eclipse
distorts the profile of the eclipsed star (Rossiter–McLaughlin effect).
KX Aqr: the TESS eclipses last to 0.065 in phase from the conjunctions,
and there the velocities are off by 2–25 km/s. `--exclude-conjunctions
PHASE` (GUI: **Exclude**) leaves out the epochs closer than PHASE to a
conjunction of the fitted orbit (ν + ω = 90° and 270°; for a circular
orbit phases 0 and 0.5 from T₀). The fit is repeated until the same
epochs stay out; an epoch at the limit that goes in and out (each choice
moves the orbit a little) is left out. The epochs left out stay in the RV
table (`used` 0) and in the figure (open symbols; beyond the residual axis
as open triangles at its edge), and the report gives their number. Take PHASE a little larger than the
half-duration of the eclipses (KX Aqr: 1/12 = 0.083).

**The RV table** `result_orbit_rv.txt`: every epoch with spectrum,
BJD_TDB, phase, `used`, `swapped` (exchanged by the swap check; the
velocities are given as used), data set, and for each star RV, its error
as given, the total error and O − C (on the zero point of the reference
data set). The orbit figure draws the total errors; in the residual panel
the measurement errors are the thick bars inside them.

  `night` +6.0, `none` +33.5.

  FEROS pull rms 0.95/0.88 — one error scale for both stars gives

**Sampler.** With a model error the posterior is sampled with
differential-evolution steps (ter Braak 2006) and 20 % stretch steps: the
autocorrelation time of LL Aqr's 11 parameters falls from about 130 to 70
steps, with the same posterior as a random-walk Metropolis chain of
4 × 10⁵ steps. emcee's `DESnookerMove` is not used: in that check it gave
errors 20 % too small.

**What the model error is not.** It is not an error that a single
spectrum can give: `bf` and `batch` still report the measurement error
(section 9). It is not an error of the orbit model either: an outer orbit
(a third star), apsidal motion or a wrong period show as structure in the
residuals, not as scatter — correct the model first (KX Aqr: γ follows the
third star, `--third-star`). The model error then shows it: the HARPS epochs of KX Aqr
alone, without the third-star term, give s1 = 1.85 and s2 = 1.64 km/s with
rho = +0.96, and `night` is the best rule (BIC) — a shift that both stars
share and that changes from night to night, i.e. the outer orbit. And it
is no remedy for swapped or blended epochs: the swap check and
`--exclude-conjunctions` take those out.

#### The third star

In a hierarchical triple the inner binary and the third star move about
their common centre of mass. Thus the systemic velocity of the binary is
not constant — it follows the third star:

```
gamma12(t_i) = gamma + beta3 (RV3_i - <RV3>),     beta3 = -M3 / (M1 + M2)
```

(conservation of momentum along the line of sight; no inclination
enters). `--third-star` (GUI: **Third-star gamma (RV3)**; `batch --orbit
--third-star` with `--components 3`) puts this term in the orbit. The
measured RV3 of each spectrum gives the outer motion, so the outer orbit
needs no model and no period of its own. β₃ is a free parameter — in the
least-squares fit, the model-error fit and the posterior (uniform prior
±5, wider when the least-squares value needs it). The report gives β₃
and M₃/(M₁+M₂) = −β₃; the posterior table has both; the RV table has RV3
and the term β₃ (RV3 − ⟨RV3⟩) of every epoch. γ is the systemic velocity
of the binary at RV3 = ⟨RV3⟩ (the `third star` line gives ⟨RV3⟩). The
term is the same for both stars: the figure and the Wilson plot show the
velocities with it taken out, as they do with the data-set offsets.

Conditions:

- RV3 must be on the velocity scale of RV1 and RV2 — the third peak of
  the same spectra (a batch with `--components 3`). The data-set offsets
  are fitted for all three stars together. An epoch without RV3 is left
  out (the `third star` line counts them).
- The error of RV3 is not propagated. It adds |β₃| × err3 to each
  velocity (KX Aqr: 0.017 km/s against errors of 0.5 km/s); the report
  gives the value, and a warning comes when it is more than 0.2 × the RV
  errors. The bias of β₃ from the RV3 errors (attenuation) is
  var(err3)/var(RV3): for KX Aqr about 10⁻⁴.
- β₃ > 0 is not the reflex motion of a third body. It comes from a zero
  point that moves all three stars together, or from wrong RV3 labels
  (another peak taken as the third star). A warning comes when β₃ > 0 by
  2σ.
- The light-travel time across the outer orbit is not modelled. It moves
  the orbital phase by up to a₁₂ sin i / c of the outer orbit; check it in
  the eclipse timings (O − C).

**Tests.**

- KX Aqr (the data of the model-error tests above,
  `--exclude-conjunctions 0.0833`, `additive`): β₃ = −0.396 ± 0.019, thus
  M₃/(M₁+M₂) = 0.40; K₁ = 110.897 ± 0.134, K₂ = 106.291 ± 0.157 km/s.
  With β₃ fixed at −0.397 and taken out before the orbit:
  K₁ = 110.898 ± 0.131, K₂ = 106.286 ± 0.154 km/s, the same K. With β₃
  free, its uncertainty goes into γ (±0.147 against ±0.127 km/s) and into

#### Reading the corner plot

The corner plot is drawn with the corner package (Foreman-Mackey
[2016](https://doi.org/10.21105/joss.00024), JOSS 1, 24), in the layout
journals use for it — e.g. Hinse et al. 2024, A&A 687, A116, Fig. C.1. Every
kept model is a complete orbit (P, T₀, e, ω, K₁, K₂, γ) that fits the
velocities about as well as the errors allow; the plot shows that cloud of
models from every side at once.

- **Diagonal: one histogram per parameter.** It counts the models at each
  value of that parameter, whatever the others do — the parameter's
  *marginal* posterior. The **three dashed lines are the 16th, 50th and 84th
  percentiles**: the middle one is the median, the reported value, and 68 %
  of the models lie between the outer two, whose distances from the middle
  one are the −/+ errors in the report. A narrow, symmetric, single-peaked
  histogram is a well-measured parameter; if one side is longer, the error
  is asymmetric and the report quotes two different numbers.
- **Below the diagonal: every pair of parameters.** The grey map is the
  density of models (darker = more), and the **three contours are the 1σ, 2σ
  and 3σ regions** — they hold 39 %, 86 % and 99 % of the models, the 2-D
  equivalents of ±1, 2, 3σ. The dots outside the outer contour are single
  models. A **round** contour means the two parameters are measured
  independently; a **tilted** one means they are **correlated**: changing one
  can be compensated by changing the other, so their errors are not
  independent. Two such correlations are normal:
  - **P and T₀** (tilted downwards when T₀ lies before the middle of the
    data, as for LL Aqr): a slightly longer period with a slightly earlier
    T₀ puts the middle of the data at the same phase.
  - **T₀ and ω** (tilted upwards) in an eccentric orbit: periastron a little
    later in time and a little further on in angle leaves the times the
    stars pass each other, which the curve fixes best, nearly unchanged.
- **P and T₀ are drawn relative to a reference value**, their median rounded
  to 4 decimals, which the axis label gives (`P - 20.1783 [d]`). ω is in
  degrees. The values themselves are not printed on the plot: they are in the
  report and the posterior table.

What a problem looks like:

- **A histogram cut off sharply on one side** — the posterior has run into
  the edge of its prior box, and the report warns. At a physical limit this
  is real: e cut at 0 means the data are consistent with a circular orbit.
  Elsewhere the box was too narrow — raise `--prior-scale`.
- **Two separate islands of contours, or a histogram with two peaks** — two
  different solutions fit about equally well (a period alias, two possible
  ω). The median then lies between them and describes neither. Fix the
  period, or add epochs.
- **Curved (banana-shaped) contours** — the model depends on the parameters
  non-linearly here, so a least-squares ± would be wrong; the posterior's is
  right. Typical for e and ω when e is small.
- **Ragged histograms and wobbly contours** — too few independent models;
  the report also warns that the chain is shorter than 50 autocorrelation
  times. Raise `--mcmc-steps`.

The derived quantities (q, M sin³i, a sin i) are not drawn, but they are
computed **from every model** and summarised the same way in the report and
the posterior table. That is why their errors are right even where the
parameters are correlated: M sin³i depends on (K₁+K₂)², on K of the other star,
on P and on e, and the models carry those dependencies along, where adding
the separate errors in quadrature would not.

#### What the parameters mean

| Parameter | Unit | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| P | d | **Orbital period**: the time for one full orbit. With `--period` it may only move within ±0.1 % of the value given. |
| T₀ | BJD_TDB | **Reference time** — reported for the first cycle at or after the first epoch (T₀ is defined only up to whole periods; its error follows the shift). Eccentric orbit: the time of **periastron**, when the stars are closest. Circular orbit: there is no periastron; ω is held at 90°, so T₀ is the time the primary passes γ going from redshift to blueshift — the conjunction with the primary behind its companion, i.e. the **primary eclipse** when the primary is the star eclipsed at Min I. It can then be compared with a photometric T₀: the difference should be a whole number of periods. |
| e | — | **Eccentricity**: 0 is a circle, near 1 a very elongated ellipse. Close binaries are often circularised by tides. It makes the RV curve lopsided rather than a sine. |
| ω | deg | **Argument of periastron of the primary**: the angle, in the orbital plane and in the direction of motion, from the point where the primary crosses the sky plane receding from us (the ascending node) to periastron. The secondary's ω is 180° more. It sets *which way* the curve is lopsided. Poorly defined when e is small (see the T₀–ω correlation above). |
| K₁, K₂ | km/s | **Semi-amplitudes**: half the peak-to-peak RV variation of each star. The more massive star moves less, so K₁/K₂ = M₂/M₁. |
| γ | km/s | **Systemic velocity**: the velocity of the binary's centre of mass, in the frame of the fitted RVs (barycentric when the correction was applied or the spectra were already barycentric). It also absorbs the template's zero point and the stars' small gravitational-redshift and convective-shift difference. |
| q = M₂/M₁ | — | **Mass ratio**, K₁/K₂ from the orbit. The Wilson plot gives an independent value with no period at all; agreement between the two is a check worth making. |
| M₁ sin³i, M₂ sin³i | M☉ | **Minimum masses**, P (K₁+K₂)² K_other (1−e²)^{3/2} / (2πG). The true mass is M sin³i / sin³i, which needs the inclination i; for an eclipsing binary i is near 90°, so sin³i is close to 1 — 0.989 at i = 85°, 0.955 at 80°, 0.901 at 75° — but take i from the light curve. |
| a₁ sin i, a₂ sin i | R☉ | **Projected semi-major axis of each star's orbit** around the centre of mass, K P √(1−e²) / (2π). |
| a sin i | R☉ | **Projected semi-major axis of the relative orbit** (star around star), the sum of the two above. Needed with the radii from a light curve, which come out as fractions of a. |
| β₃ | — | With `--third-star`: **how the binary's γ follows the third star**, γ₁₂ = γ + β₃ (RV3 − ⟨RV3⟩). For a third body β₃ = −M₃/(M₁+M₂). |
| M₃/(M₁+M₂) | — | With `--third-star`: −β₃, the mass of the third star over the binary's mass. No inclination enters (the velocities along the line of sight give the momentum along it). |

#### The posterior table

`result_orbit_posterior.txt` holds the numbers behind the corner plot, one row
per parameter (the derived ones too), as plain columns to read with any
program:

| Column | Content |
|---|---|
| `parameter` | Name with unit: `P[d]`, `T0[BJD]`, `e`, `omega[deg]`, `K1[km/s]`, `K2[km/s]`, `gamma[km/s]`, the offsets, `s1[km/s]`, `s2[km/s]` and `rho` (with a model error), `q`, `M1sin3i[Msun]`, `M2sin3i[Msun]`, `a1sini[Rsun]`, `a2sini[Rsun]`, `asini[Rsun]`; `beta3` and `M3/(M1+M2)` with `--third-star`; `e` and `omega` only for an eccentric fit |
| `median`, `err_minus`, `err_plus` | The posterior median — the value to quote — and its distances down to the 16th and up to the 84th percentile |
| `prior_min`, `prior_max` | The edges of the uniform prior box the sampler was confined to (`nan` for a derived quantity, which has no prior of its own) |

```bash
python rv_analysis.py header --spectrum ADP.xxxx.fits --site lasilla
```

The output is the standard summary block below, followed by the header
cards themselves: every HDU of a FITS file, each under a `--- HDU k: NAME
(type) ---` line, or the comment header of an ASCII table and its PolarBase
`.meta` sidecar. (The GUI's **Header** window puts the two on separate tabs,
**Cards** and **Summary**.)

`--output` saves everything to a text file; `--resolve` asks SIMBAD for the
coordinates of the OBJECT the file names, when the file itself gives none
(the command is otherwise offline); an ESO OBJECT such as `2234_LL-Aqr`
is looked up as LL Aqr. Without coordinates there is no BJD_TDB
and no v_bary to print, so `--resolve` is what completes the summary for a
file that carries a target and a time but no position.

#### The standard summary block

**Every spectrum prints the same block, in the same order, whatever it is.**
A HARPS phase-3 product, a SOPHIE `s1d`, a FEROS or UVES archive file, a raw
CCD frame, a normalized ASCII table and a file whose only metadata is its
name all come out looking like this — and a field the file does not provide
is printed as `-` rather than left out, so a missing value is *visibly*
missing:

```
=============== SPECTRUM HEADER =============
File       : ADP.2014-09-24T09:43:51.650.fits
Format     : FITS, 2 HDUs: binary table (WAVE, FLUX, ERR)
Instrument : HARPS   (INSTRUME)   [built-in: R = 115000]
Telescope  : ESO-3.6   (TELESCOP)
Target     : LL-Aqr   (OBJECT)
RV info    : ESO DRS BERV = -4.2699   (Barycentric Earth Radial Velocity)
DATE-OBS   : 2010-09-10T00:17:46.910   (DATE-OBS)
RA [deg]   : 338.67179   (RA)
Dec [deg]  : -3.59881
EXPTIME [s]: 300.0019   (EXPTIME)
Site       : lon -70.7345 E, lat -29.2584, 2400 m   (HIERARCH ESO TEL GEOLON/…)
Site used  : Observatory from the file header (…)  [ESO La Silla]
BJD_TDB    : 2455449.520562   (mid-exposure, computed here)
v_bary     : -4.2672 km/s   (computed here)
cross-check: pipeline BERV = -4.2699 km/s, ours -4.2672 (+3 m/s) - OK
Bary frame : corrected (BARYCENT), v_bary = -4.2699 km/s
=============================================
```

The part in brackets after each value is **where it was read from** — the
card, the comment key, or the naming convention. That is the point of the
block: the commonest header problem is not a missing value but a value taken
from the wrong place, and a summary that only prints `OBJECT: LL-Aqr` cannot
show you that. `Format` names the layout the reader actually made of the
file, and `Instrument` is looked up in the built-in
[spectrograph table](#6-instruments-and-observatories) so you can see at once
whether `--spectrograph` will be recognized.

Each field is filled from the first source that has it:

| Field | Read from |
|---|---|
| Instrument | `INSTRUME`, `INSTRUM`, `INSTRUMENT`, `ESO INS ID`, `ESO SEQ ARM`; the `OHP …` keyword prefix identifies SOPHIE, which names itself in no card; the Neo-Narval sequence cards identify Neo-Narval |
| Telescope | `TELESCOP`, `TELESCOPE`, `ESO TEL NAME` |
| Target | `OBJECT`, `OBJNAME` (Demetra), `TARGNAME`, `TARGET`, `ESO OBS TARG NAME`, `OHP TARG NAME`, else the PolarBase `.meta` (`Object name`), else the file name |
| DATE-OBS | `DATE-OBS`, `DATE_OBS`, `OHP OBS DATE START`, else the PolarBase `.meta` (`Observation date`), else the file name |
| RA/Dec | `RA`/`DEC`, `OBJCTRA`/`OBJCTDEC`, `RA-OBJ`/`DEC-OBJ`, PolarBase `RA_TARG`/`DEC_TARG`, or the packed sexagesimal `OHP TARG ALPHA`/`DELTA`; else the PolarBase `.meta` (`RA`/`DEC`); `--resolve` falls back to SIMBAD |
| EXPTIME | `EXPTIME`, `EXPOSURE`, `ITIME`, `TELAPSE`, else the PolarBase `.meta` (`Texposure`), else the file name |
| Site | ESO `TEL GEOLON/…`, Neo-Narval `LONGITUD/…`, `GEOLON/…`, `OBSGEO-L/…`, `LONG-OBS/…`, `SITELON/…` (written by `normalize`), `OBS-LONG/…`, Demetra `GEO_LONG/…` and `BSS_LONG/…`; then the instrument's home observatory; then the file name |
| Epoch | The note explaining what the timestamp *is* — exposure start, mid-exposure, or the mid-time of a sub-exposure sequence — for the conventions where that differs |
| Bary frame | The keyword vocabulary of [section 7](#7-barycentric-frame-what-the-headers-say-and-who-decides) |
| PolarBase | Printed for PolarBase spectra only: `normalized` (`<odo>i`) or `not-normalized` (`<odo>i_u`), from `PBPRODUCT` (written by `normalize`), `FLUX_CAL` (`.fts`), the `.s` title line, `.meta` `Normalized`, or the `_u` in the name, in that order |

The packed sexagesimal cards deserve a note: `OHP TARG ALPHA = 112631.39`
means 11:26:31.39, not 112631 degrees. Those are decoded by the reader that
owns them and are deliberately *not* in the generic list, because a number
read from the wrong convention gives a position that is wrong without ever
looking wrong.

#### Normalized files keep everything

`normalize` writes the metadata it found into the `# KEY = VALUE` comment
header of its output, and `header` reads all of it back, so **a normalized
copy reports the same block as the file it came from** — same instrument,
telescope, target, epoch, coordinates, exposure time, site, BJD, v_bary and
bary-frame verdict, differing only in `Format` and in the source labels:

```
Instrument : HARPS   (INSTRUME)   [built-in: R = 115000]     <- FITS parent
Instrument : HARPS   (INSTRUME)   [built-in: R = 115000]     <- _norm.txt
Site       : … (HIERARCH ESO TEL GEOLON/…)                   <- FITS parent
Site       : … (SITELON/SITELAT/SITEALT)                     <- _norm.txt
```

That matters because the normalized ASCII file is often the only copy that
survives a project. Free-text lines are carried too: the sentence explaining
a Neo-Narval sequence epoch (`# Neo-Narval: epoch = mid of the 4-exposure
sequence …`) is read back as the `Epoch` line rather than being discarded as
an unrecognized card.

#### Metadata in a sidecar file: PolarBase

A PolarBase `.s` table has a single title line and no header; everything
else is in the `.meta` file of `Key = value` lines delivered beside it.
`header`, `ccf`, `bf`, `todcor`, `batch` and `normalize` read that file for
any ASCII spectrum without a header of its own. That covers the `.s` and
also a wavelength window cut out of it:

- the `.meta` with the spectrum's own name (`897395i_u.s` →
  `897395i_u.meta`) is used first;
- otherwise, when the folder holds exactly one `.meta`, it is taken to
  describe every spectrum in that folder. A 6-digit `HHMMSS` in the file
  name that differs from the `.meta` time refuses the match
  (`4200_4500_073357.txt` matches `Observation date = 2007-02-28 07:33:57`);
- **failing both, the folders around the file are searched** — its own
  directory and the one above it, each with their immediate
  subdirectories, which is the shape of a PolarBase download: one folder
  per exposure under one star folder. A `.meta` there is accepted only
  when its `Observation date` matches the `HHMMSS` in the file name and
  every candidate that matches agrees on epoch, coordinates and exposure
  time (the `i` and `i_u` sidecars of one exposure agree on all three, so
  they count as one; two different exposures do not, and the file is left
  without metadata rather than given the wrong one);
- **a normalized copy asks the file it was made from.** `normalize`
  writes `; from <path>` into the comment header of its output, and when
  the copy is missing a field the source file — and whatever `.meta`
  sits near *it* — supplies it. This is what makes a cut normalized into
  a results directory work: `batch` queues
  `results/Normalization/4200_4500_073357_norm.txt`, the source is
  `HD47961_EsPaDOnS_All/4200_4500_073357.txt`, and the sidecar is in
  `2007-02-28 073357/` one level above that. The recovered target, epoch,
  coordinates, exposure time, instrument (hence the observatory) and
  wavelength frame are all labelled with where they came from;
- a card written in the spectrum file itself always wins over the `.meta`.

The source label names the file (`PolarBase 897395i_u.meta: Observation
date`), and `header` prints the `.meta` in full after the summary block. The
catalogue `Radial Velocity` line in it is SIMBAD's value for the star, not a
measurement of this spectrum, and is not read.

```
Format     : PolarBase / Libre-ESpRIT table ('ESPaDOnS NOTnormalized spectrum'); metadata in 897395i_u.meta
Target     : HD 47961   (PolarBase 897395i_u.meta: Object name)
DATE-OBS   : 2007-02-28T07:33:57   (PolarBase 897395i_u.meta: Observation date)
EXPTIME [s]: 900.0   (PolarBase 897395i_u.meta: Texposure)
Epoch      : PolarBase .meta: 'Observation date' is the UT START of the exposure (it equals 'Julian Date' = 2454159.8152430556); Texposure = 900 s, so the mid-exposure epoch is 450 s later
Site used  : Observatory from the instrument (ESPADONS): cfht (lon -155.4685 E, lat +19.8253, 4204 m). …
Bary frame : corrected (HELIOCEN)
PolarBase  : not-normalized   (title '*** ESPaDOnS NOTnormalized spectrum')   [no pipeline telluric correction; do not mix with normalized 'i' epochs]
```

#### Metadata in the file name

Not every reduction writes a header, and one of them writes a FITS file whose
header has been stripped to the wavelength WCS and nothing else. For those
products the file name is not decoration — it is the only metadata there is,
and RVCalPy reads it, in `header`, in `ccf`/`bf`/`todcor` and in `batch`.

Two rules hold for every convention below:

- **a card actually written in the file always wins over the name**;
- **a name may be trusted for what its own convention encodes and nothing
  more.** The DAO pattern is that observatory's own, so it implies the site;
  the Demetra pattern is a vendor default that every telescope running that
  software writes, so it implies no site whatever and does not pretend to.

##### DAO

The DAO (Dominion Astrophysical Observatory) products are plain three-column
ASCII — wavelength [Å], normalized flux, error — with nothing above the first
data row, and everything a FITS header would have held is in the name:

```
sci_KLPer_JD2458010.851821111_DO2017-09-14T080137_ID16030_wst.nrm
 |    |     |                  |                   |       |
 |    |     |                  |                   |       processing tags
 |    |     |                  |                   frame number
 |    |     |                  UT date/time the exposure STARTED
 |    |     mid-exposure JD (UTC, geocentric)
 |    target
 frame kind
```

A file matching this pattern gets its OBJECT, its epoch **and its
observatory** from the name, and the target name is resolved to coordinates
so the barycentric correction can be computed at all.

**The two times in the name are different times.** `JD...` is the
mid-exposure epoch, `DO...` the moment the shutter opened, so their
difference is half the exposure; RVCalPy uses the JD as the epoch and
reports EXPTIME as 0, because adding half an exposure to a mid-exposure
time would shift it twice. It also checks the two against each other and
says so if they disagree by more than a plausible half-exposure — then one
of them is not what the convention says it is, and you should pass
`--obstime` yourself.

That JD is a **plain UTC geocentric date, not a BJD**: across the three
KL Per frames of 2017-09-04/14/15 its offset from the DO stamp is 1500.0 s
every time, constant to better than half a second over eleven nights, which
is a fixed half-exposure and not a light-travel term — a heliocentric
correction would have drifted by up to a minute and a half over that span.
So the barycentric correction still has to be applied to the epoch.

Two cautions, both because a file name is weaker evidence than a header:

- **The observatory comes from the naming convention alone.** It is used
  only after the file's own header and its INSTRUME card have come up empty,
  it is always announced in the output, and `--site` overrides it. A renamed
  or borrowed file would carry it wrongly.
- **The wavelength frame is unrecorded.** The header check of
  [section 7](#7-barycentric-frame-what-the-headers-say-and-who-decides) reads
  headers, and there is no header here, so it can only return *unknown* —
  which is not the same as *topocentric*. A run that applies the correction
  therefore warns that it is doing so on no evidence. Check one epoch
  against an RV you trust: **if the uncorrected value is the one that
  matches, the spectra are already barycentric and you want `--no-bary`.**
  For the KL Per frames above that is exactly the case — the measured RVs
  reproduce the published table to 0.2–1.6 km/s with `--no-bary`, and are
  off by the full v_bary (+22 to +24 km/s) without it.

##### Demetra (Shelyak)

Demetra names every frame it writes after the target, the frame kind, the
exposure time and the UT start of the exposure, and its extracted products
inherit that name:

```
HD234899_Object_3599s_20250809_215627-1_mbtsf_ext_wav_rv_dop.fits
 |        |      |     |        |      |  |
 |        |      |     |        |      |  processing chain
 |        |      |     |        |      exposure number of the night
 |        |      |     |        UT time the exposure STARTED
 |        |      |     UT date
 |        |      exposure time in seconds
 |        frame kind (Object, Flat, Bias, ThAr, …)
 target
```

This one matters even for FITS: the extracted `.fits` above holds `CRVAL1`,
`CDELT1`, `CTYPE1` **and nothing else** — no OBJECT, no DATE-OBS, no exposure
time, no site. RVCalPy takes the target, the epoch and EXPTIME from the name;
`TIC142587827_Object_3600s_20231014_191542_order_all_norm.txt` is read the
same way.

**The timestamp is the UT START of the exposure**, so EXPTIME is carried
along with it and the usual half-exposure is added where it always is. That
is not an inference — the raw frame that still has its header says so. For
`HD47961_Object_3590s_20241109_013040-1` Demetra wrote

```
DATE-OBS= '2024-11-09T01:30:41' / Date of observation start
EXPTIME =                 3590 / [s] Total observation duration
JD_OBS  = '2460623.563'        / JD start observation
JD_MID  = '2460623.5838'       / JD mid observation
```

— the name's `013040` is that DATE-OBS to the second and its `3590s` is that
EXPTIME exactly, and `JD_MID − JD_OBS` is half of it. When a header does
carry `JD_MID`, RVCalPy checks it against DATE-OBS + EXPTIME/2 and says so if
they disagree by more than a minute; it does not otherwise use it, because
DATE-OBS + EXPTIME/2 is exact to the second where `JD_MID` is rounded to
1e-4 d = 8.6 s.

Two cautions:

- **The name does not give the observatory** and RVCalPy will not invent one.
  Demetra is Shelyak Instruments' software, used the world over, so the
  pattern says nothing about where the spectrum was taken; without `--site`
  the run falls back to the default observatory and says so. For the AUKR
  T80 / Whoppshel spectra above that is `--site kreiken` — and their *raw*
  `.fit` frames give it directly, in `GEO_LONG`/`GEO_LAT`/`GEO_ELEV` and
  `BSS_LONG`/`BSS_LAT`/`BSS_ELEV`, which RVCalPy reads.
- **The processing tags are not header cards.** A name ending
  `_rv_dop` *may* mean the wavelengths have already been Doppler-corrected,
  and RVCalPy points at the tag when it is about to apply a correction to a
  file with no recorded frame — but it does not act on it. Settle it the way
  the DAO case above was settled: measure one epoch both ways and keep the
  one that matches an RV you trust.

##### An instrument named in the file name

Weakest of all, and last to be consulted. A file that has no header and
follows no convention still sometimes states the one thing plainly:

```
ESPaDOnS_Procyon-1.txt
```

If a whole word of the name is a spectrograph in the
[built-in table](#6-instruments-and-observatories), RVCalPy takes the
instrument from it — and, only when nothing else has supplied one, that
instrument's home observatory, announced as the weakest evidence it uses.
The match must be a complete token, so `template_5215K_Logg3.txt` and
`ADP.2016-09-26T07:15:37.665.fits` match nothing; and the three instrument
names that are also ordinary English words — `MIKE`, `FIRE`, `GHOST` — must
be capitalized the way the table spells them, so `my_fire_test.dat` is not
read as a Magellan spectrum and sent to Las Campanas.

**It gives you the instrument, not the epoch.** A name like that records no
date, no time and no exposure, so there is no BJD and no barycentric
correction to be had from it — pass `--object` (or `--ra/--dec`) and
`--obstime` yourself. The summary says so instead of showing a bare dash:

```
Instrument : ESPaDOnS   (file name ('ESPaDOnS'))   [built-in: R = 68000]
Target     : -
DATE-OBS   : -
BJD_TDB    : -   (needs coordinates and an epoch: give --object NAME,
                  or --ra/--dec; --obstime)
```

RVCalPy does **not** guess the target from the rest of the name. `Procyon` is
obvious to you and not to a parser: the same rule applied to
`example_BF_HARPS_norm.txt` would offer `example` or `BF`, and a wrong name
that happens to resolve in SIMBAD gives coordinates that are wrong without
ever looking wrong.


### `fetch-adp` — download reduced ESO spectra

Raw ESO archive downloads often contain only the CCD exposures
(`HARPS.<date>.fits`, `FEROS.<date>.fits`, 2148×4096 frames) even though the
archive also holds their pipeline-reduced, wavelength-calibrated phase-3
products (`ADP.<date>.fits`). `fetch-adp` bridges the gap without leaving the
terminal:

```bash
python rv_analysis.py fetch-adp --spectra 'data/HARPS.*.fits' [--dry-run]
```

For every raw file it reads `INSTRUME`, `RA`/`DEC` and `MJD-OBS` from the
header, queries the ESO TAP service (`ivoa.ObsCore`) for reduced spectra of
that instrument within `--radius` (default 30″) of the pointing, picks the
product whose start time matches `MJD-OBS` to within `--tmatch` (default
120 s), and downloads it from the ESO dataportal into the raw file's own
directory (change with `--dest`). Products already on disk are skipped, so
the command is safe to re-run; `--dry-run` only reports what would be
fetched. Anonymous access works for all public data; queries and downloads
are retried automatically because the ESO service intermittently drops
connections.

## 6. Instruments and observatories

- **Spectrograph** — `--spectrograph NAME` (or the GUI dropdown) sets the
  resolving power R automatically. The built-in list covers the common
  optical/NIR échelle, mid- and low-resolution instruments (HARPS, HARPS-N,
  ESPRESSO, UVES, FEROS, SOPHIE, CORALIE, PEPSI, NARVAL, ESPaDOnS, MIKE, PFS,
  GHOST, STELES, CHIRON, DAO, CRIRES+, NIRPS, WINERED, FLAMES-GIRAFFE,
  X-shooter, MagE, M2FS, IFUM, GMOS, SOXS, FIRE, FORS2, MUSE, LDSS3, EFOSC2,
  KMOS, Flamingos-2, TripleSpec, …) plus the **Gaia RVS** (R = 11 500). Print
  the full table with `python rv_analysis.py --list-spectrographs`, or set R
  directly with `--resolution`.
- **Observatory** — `--site` accepts a built-in observatory name
  (`paranal`, `lasilla`, `ctio`, `ohp`, `tug`, `dag`, `kreiken`, `lco`, `soar`,
  `tng`, `dao`, …), a `lonE,lat,alt` string in degrees/metres for a site not
  in the list, or an astropy `of_site` name. The coordinates (longitude
  East-positive) drive the barycentric correction, including the diurnal
  term, matching the MIDAS `comp/bary` convention. List the built-in sites
  with `python rv_analysis.py --list-observatories`.

  **Leave it out and the observatory is read from the file itself** —
  `LONGITUD/LATITUDE/OBSELEV` (Neo-Narval), `ESO TEL GEOLON/GEOLAT/GEOELEV`,
  `GEOLON/GEOLAT/GEOELEV`, `OBSGEO-*`, `LONG-OBS/LAT-OBS/ALT-OBS` — and the
  line

  ```
  Observatory from the file header (LONGITUD/LATITUDE/OBSELEV): lon +0.1333 E, lat +42.9333, 2869 m  [TBL (Pic du Midi)]
  ```

  says which one was used. Only when no header gives a position does the
  default `paranal` apply, and then it says so. This matters more than it
  looks: Earth rotation alone is ±0.46 km/s, so analysing a Pic-du-Midi
  spectrum with the Paranal default costs 0.4 km/s — more than everything
  else in the pipeline is trying to get right, with nothing in the numbers
  to give it away. An explicit `--site` always wins.

Giving a resolution matters for more than the plot: in BF mode it sets the
smoothing FWHM **and** the default velocity step, and it defines the
"unresolved" limit of the sampling guards
([section 9](#9-bf-velocity-step-and-the-reliability-of-the-rv-errors)).

## 7. Barycentric frame: what the headers say, and who decides

Whether a reduced spectrum's wavelengths are still topocentric or already
shifted to the Solar-System barycentre is not always obvious — HARPS and
FEROS products from the ESO archive, for instance, arrive already
corrected, while many instrument-pipeline intermediate products do not.
Applying the correction to an already-corrected file doubles it. The
`barycheck` command decides from the FITS headers of **all** given files:

```bash
python rv_analysis.py barycheck --spectra 'HARPS/ADP.*.fits' 'FEROS/ADP.*.fits'
```

The bundled examples show both cases: the HARPS and SOPHIE spectra are
already barycentric, so `ccf`/`bf` **warn** when run on them without
`--no-bary` (they run, and v_bary is added — see *Who decides* below), while
the Neo-Narval spectrum is topocentric and the correction is applied
silently ([section 2](#2-quick-start)). Nothing but the file
headers decides that.

**Neo-Narval / NARVAL / Libre-ESpRIT** files get a verdict of their own:
the pipeline writes `BERV` and `BTLA` into the header but leaves the
wavelengths in the observer frame, so the correction must be applied. That
is checked against the data rather than assumed — telluric lines are fixed
in the observer frame, and in the O₂ A band (7594–7660 Å) two epochs whose
BERV differs by 18.5 km/s put them at the same place to 0.2 km/s, where a
barycentric scale would have moved them by the full 18.5 km/s.

**PolarBase (ESPaDOnS / NARVAL `<odometer>i.s`, `<odometer>i_u.s`)** is the opposite case:
its wavelengths are **already heliocentric**, so use `--no-bary` (untick the
box in the GUI). The `.s` table has no header of its own. Target,
coordinates, `Observation date` (the UT **start** of the exposure),
`Texposure` and the instrument are in the `.meta` file beside it, and
`header`, `ccf`/`bf`/`todcor`, `batch` and `normalize` read them from there
([details](#metadata-in-a-sidecar-file-polarbase)):

- the `.meta` with the same name as the spectrum is used when it exists;
- otherwise, when a folder holds exactly **one** `.meta`, that file
  describes every spectrum in the folder, so a window you cut out of the
  `.s` (e.g. `4200_4500_073357.txt`) gets the same epoch. A 6-digit
  `HHMMSS` in the file name that differs from the `.meta` time refuses the
  match;
- a spectrum copied into a folder with no `.meta` (or with several) has no
  epoch. Normalize it in its own folder first: the normalized copy carries
  `OBJECT`, `DATE-OBS`, `EXPTIME`, `RA`/`DEC`, the CFHT site and
  `SPECSYS = HELIOCEN` in its header, and can then go anywhere.

The frame was measured on HD 47961, not assumed. The O₂ A, B and γ telluric
bands of `897395i_u.s` (2007-02-28) and `1522826i_u.s` (2012-01-31) are
offset by +10.04 km/s, and the v_bary difference of the two epochs is
+10.19 km/s. On a topocentric scale they would coincide.

**PolarBase `.fts`** (`<odometer>i.fts`, `<odometer>i_u.fts`) is the same
spectrum as the `.s`, as a VO FITS table (`AWAV` in nm, `FLUX_NOR`,
`FLUX_ERR`), and is read directly with no `.meta` needed: `OBJECT`,
`RA_TARG`/`DEC_TARG`, `DATE-OBS`, `TELAPSE`, `INSTRUME` and
`SPECSYS = HELIOCENTER`. **Its `DATE-OBS` comment says "Middle date-time of
acquisition", but the value is the exposure START.** It equals `JDUTC`,
`TMID` and the "Time of observations" in the pipeline log (`.out`), so
RVCalPy adds `TELAPSE`/2 as for any start epoch. The file's other date
cards only repeat that same JD; none of them is read.

**Never mix the two PolarBase products in one RV series.** A download holds
each exposure twice: `<odo>i` (normalized by the pipeline, which also
applies its "radial velocity correction from telluric lines") and `<odo>i_u`
(not normalized). Their wavelength zero points differ: on HD 47961
2007-02-28 the two scales differ by 0.153 km/s and their telluric bands by
0.14–0.17 km/s. `header` prints which product a file is (`PolarBase :
normalized / not-normalized`, with the evidence: `FLUX_CAL`, the `.s` title
line, `.meta Normalized`, or the `_u` in the name). `normalize` carries it
forward as `PBPRODUCT`, so a re-normalized `i_u` cut still says what it is.
`batch` prints a warning, and the GUI batch asks before running, when a
queue mixes the two. Re-normalizing an `i_u` spectrum yourself does not give
it the pipeline's telluric zero point.

**Heliocentric is not barycentric, and the difference is added
automatically.** PolarBase corrects to the Sun, not to the solar-system
barycentre (Petit et al. 2014, PASP 126, 469). The two differ by the Sun's
own motion about the barycentre, at most ~13 m/s, and it changes with the
positions of Jupiter and Saturn. When a spectrum's frame is `HELIOCEN` or
`HELIOCENTER` and
the run uses `--no-bary`, `ccf`, `bf`, `todcor`, `batch` and the GUI add
v_bary − v_helio at mid-exposure to every reported RV. The term is printed
and written in a `helio_to_bary[km/s]` column. For HD 47961 at CFHT it is
+12.2 m/s on 2007-02-28 and −3.8 m/s on 2012-01-31. The telluric lines cannot
confirm a term this small: they scatter by 50–220 m/s around the expected
correction, which is the real zero-point limit of these spectra. With
`--force-bary` the term is not added, because the full v_bary is.

The PolarBase pipeline log (`<odo>i.out`) states the velocity it applied,
"Heliocentric velocity of observer towards star". On 897395i it is
−24.952 km/s, which equals our heliocentric correction at the **end** of the
900 s exposure (−24.9520), not at mid-exposure (−24.9371). RVCalPy does not
read the `.out`, so that 15 m/s is not corrected. The same log's HJD
uses a light-travel time 44 s shorter than astropy's, so do not take the HJD
from it.

**v_bary belongs to mid-exposure, like the BJD.** `DATE-OBS` is the moment
the shutter opened; the velocity the spectrum carries is the one averaged
over the exposure, so half of `EXPTIME` is added before v_bary is computed —
the same shift the BJD gets, so the correction and the BJD printed beside
it describe the same instant. It is worth having: v_bary drifts by up to
2.4 m/s per minute, which is ~15 m/s over half a 900 s ESPaDOnS exposure
and ~42 m/s over half a 2700 s UVES one. On the bundled examples our
mid-exposure v_bary agrees with the DRS's own BERV to +6 m/s (HARPS) and
+4 m/s (SOPHIE); computed at the exposure start it would differ by +11 and
+29 m/s.

A time given as a JD/BJD number is already a mid-exposure quantity by
construction and is never shifted.

**The pipeline's own numbers are used as a check.** When a file carries a
`BERV`-type card, our barycentric velocity is compared with it, and when a
Neo-Narval file carries `BTLA`, our BJD is compared with that:

```
  cross-check: pipeline BERV = -29.0699 km/s, ours -29.0640 (+6 m/s) - OK [ours recomputed at the sequence start, the epoch Libre-ESpRIT's BERV refers to]
  cross-check: BTLA (header, sequence start) = 2460842.388544; our mid-exposure BJD is +19.6 min later, inside the 41.7 min sequence - OK
```

**In a Libre-ESpRIT file `BERV` is the barycentric Earth radial velocity
[km/s] at the START of the sequence**, exactly as its own comment says
(`Barycentric Earth Rad Vel(km/s) at start`) and the same epoch `BTLA`
refers to; the header also carries `BERV_END` and `BTLA_END` for the other
end. Our v_bary is a **mid-exposure** quantity, so the two are not the same
number and must not be compared directly: v_bary drifts by up to ~2.4 m/s
per minute (0.46 km/s of diurnal term over 24 h, plus the orbital term), so
across half of a 4×616 s sequence the difference reaches ~50 m/s — the size
of the warning threshold itself. The check therefore **recomputes our value
at the sequence start** before comparing, and says so in the message. If the
start epoch or the coordinates are unavailable it falls back to the
mid-exposure value and widens the tolerance by the expected drift instead,
which it also states. Other instruments are unaffected.

A disagreement above 50 m/s is a warning naming the three things that can
cause it, in the order worth checking — observatory, coordinates, epoch:

```
WARNING: our barycentric velocity (-28.7204 km/s) differs from the pipeline's own
BERV (-29.1240 km/s) by +0.4037 km/s. Check, in this order: the observatory
(--site; a wrong one costs up to 0.46 km/s), the coordinates (--ra/--dec vs the
header) and the epoch (--obstime).
```

(that one is a real run of the same file forced to `--site paranal`). A
value that matches after a sign flip is reported as a differing *convention*,
not as an error.

Different observatories name the correction differently, so the check
matches the headers against a **keyword vocabulary** collected from the
big spectrum archives (the `BARY_*` tables in `rv_analysis.py`, which can
be extended for a new instrument). Every HDU header is scanned, in order of authority:

1. **Reference-frame keywords** `SPECSYS` / `SPECSYS1..3` / `SSYSOBS` /
   `SSYSSRC` / `VFRAME` / `WAVEFRAM` (FITS WCS standard): a value of
   `BARYCENT` or `HELIOCEN`/`HELIOCENTER` → the wavelengths are already
   corrected (a heliocentric value also triggers the helio→barycentric
   term, see below); `SOURCE` → they are in the star's rest frame, which is
   what the `result_*_shifted*.fits` files carry;
   `TOPOCENT` / `GEOCENTR` → they are not. Decisive when present.
2. **Processing switches** proving the correction was applied or
   skipped: HST STIS/COS `HELCORR` (`COMPLETE` = applied, `OMIT` =
   skipped), IRAF `DOPCOR` (written when the shift is applied),
   `BARYDONE` / `HELDONE`.
3. **`PRODCATG = SCIENCE.SPECTRUM`** — ESO phase-3 1D science products
   (`ADP.*.fits`) are barycentric by the ESO Science Data Product
   standard; used as supporting evidence when no explicit frame keyword
   exists.
4. **Pipeline velocity cards** — their value (km/s) is listed for
   information; presence alone only proves the pipeline *computed* the
   velocity, not that it applied it. Hierarchical prefixes
   (`HIERARCH ESO QC/DRS/VRAD`, `CARACAL`, ...) and underscores are
   stripped before matching, and yearly-maximum/list cards (`BERVMX`,
   ...) are ignored. Currently recognized names:

   | Keyword | Convention |
   |---|---|
   | `BERV` | HARPS / HARPS-N / SOPHIE / CORALIE DRS, ESPRESSO & NIRPS QC, CARMENES CARACAL, MAROON-X, GRACES; Libre-ESpRIT (NARVAL / Neo-Narval / ESPaDOnS), where it is the value **at the start of the sequence**, not at mid-exposure (`BERV_END` holds the other end) |
   | `BARYCORR` | FEROS FERN DRS; generic BARYCORR convention |
   | `BARYCOR`, `HELICOR` | ESO UVES / GIRAFFE / X-shooter `QC VRAD BARYCOR` / `HELICOR` |
   | `BCV`, `HCV` | IRAF rvsao `bcvcorr`, CfA/TRES, CHIRON |
   | `VHELIO` | IRAF `rvcorrect`, SDSS APOGEE apVisit |
   | `HELIO_RV` | SDSS spPlate, LAMOST |
   | `HELIOVEL` | Keck HIRES makee |
   | `SSBRV` | NEID (solar-system-barycentre RV) |
   | `SSBVEL` | LBT PEPSI |
   | `BSS_VHEL` | BeSS spectra |
   | `BVC` | HPF, iSHELL-style pipelines |
   | `VELOSYS` | FITS WCS standard frame velocity (m/s, converted) |
   | `BARYVEL`, `BARYRV`, `BARYVCOR`, `VBARY`, `HELVEL`, `VCORR` | generic velocity-correction cards |

5. **ESO-MIDAS descriptor blocks** (old FEROS / ESO 1.52m products,
   `ORIGIN = ESO-MIDAS`): descriptors such as `BARY_CORR` live inside
   HISTORY lines (`'BARY_CORR' ,'R*4 ', ...` followed by the value);
   the value is parsed out, and for FEROS the verdict is *corrected* —
   the FEROS-DRS (MIDAS) pipeline delivers barycentric-corrected
   wavelengths by default. Caveat: that pipeline's correction is only
   accurate to ~60 m/s and imprints an artificial one-year period
   (Müller et al. 2013, A&A 556, A3) — irrelevant for km/s-scale binary
   work, fatal for m/s exoplanet work.
6. **HISTORY/COMMENT** lines mentioning a barycentric or heliocentric
   correction.
7. **Julian dates are not frame evidence.** `BJD`, `BJD_TDB`,
   `OHP DRS BJD` and similar cards say that the
   **observation time** was referred to the solar-system barycentre. That
   is a different correction from v_bary, done for a different reason
   (light travel time, in seconds of date) and it leaves the wavelength
   scale untouched. `barycheck` lists these cards marked as time stamps —
   they are listed because RVCalPy uses them, cross-checking its own
   BJD_TDB against a pipeline's BJD — and they contribute **nothing** to
   the verdict: they can raise neither "corrected" nor "not-corrected",
   and they are never read as a velocity. Any other date card in a
   header is not matched by this table or by any other in this section,
   so it is neither read nor reported.

**Normalized ASCII spectra keep the evidence.** The `normalize` command
copies the barycentric-frame evidence of its source file into the
`# KEY = VALUE` comment header of the output — `INSTRUME`, the frame
keyword as `SPECSYS`, the pipeline velocity as `BERV`, or (when the
source had no explicit frame keyword, e.g. only the phase-3 `PRODCATG`
rule) a `BARYFRAME = corrected / not-corrected` summary card. Both
`barycheck` and the automatic check inside `batch` parse ASCII comment
headers with the same vocabulary, so the check keeps working after
normalization:

```bash
python rv_analysis.py normalize --spectrum ADP.xxxx.fits --output spec_norm.txt
python rv_analysis.py barycheck --spectra spec_norm.txt   # still says: corrected
```

ASCII files whose header carries no such cards (e.g. files normalized by
other tools) come back as *unknown*. Retrofit them in place with `--from-fits`: each ASCII
file is matched to its original FITS by name (the `_norm` suffix is
ignored) and the original's evidence is written into its comment header:

```bash
python rv_analysis.py barycheck --spectra '*_norm.txt' \
    --from-fits 'archive_dir/ADP.*.fits'
```

**Who decides: you.** When the header check concludes the wavelengths are
already barycentric and the correction is about to be applied anyway,
every mode **warns and carries on**, because the evidence behind a
"corrected" verdict is often indirect — a pipeline rule (what
Libre-ESpRIT, the HARPS DRS or PolarBase *usually* do), a `SPECSYS` card
copied through a reduction, or nothing more than a Julian date, which
says nothing about wavelengths at all (rule 7 above). A
program that cannot be overruled by the person holding the data turns a
guess into a veto.

- `batch` and the single-spectrum `ccf` / `bf` / `todcor` commands print
  a `WARNING:` naming the double correction, the time-stamp trap and the
  `barycheck` command, then measure and **add v_bary** as asked.
  `--force-bary` only silences that warning;
- in the **GUI**, *Apply barycentric correction* stays enabled and keeps
  whatever you set. The grey note beside it reports what the selected
  spectrum claims: *header says already corrected — check first* or
  *header says topocentric*;
- the **GUI batch** asks once before a queue whose headers disagree with
  the tick box, and runs as ticked if you confirm. It never unticks
  the box for you, and a queue that mixes frames is a question, not a
  refusal;
- the **terminal wizard** always asks "Apply the barycentric correction?"
  and only changes the *default* answer (`n`) when the header says the
  spectrum is already corrected.

So a spectrum whose header carries a **BJD** — and nothing else — is
measured, v_bary is computed for the mid-exposure epoch and added to
the RV, and both numbers appear in the result file (`RV (uncorrected)`,
`RV (barycentric corrected)`, `v_bary`), as they do for any topocentric
file. Check one epoch against an RV you trust before running a series:
if the header is right after all, the series carries v_bary twice, and a
doubled correction looks exactly like a 20–30 km/s orbit with a period of
one year.

Whatever the mode, the header-check verdict is written into the result
file as an info line, e.g.
`# barycentric frame (header check: corrected (BARYCENT), header
v_bary = -28.3379 km/s)` in `result_BF.txt` / `result_CCF.txt`, and
`# barycentric frame (header check): 25 already corrected, 0
topocentric, 0 unknown of 25 files` in `result_RV_curve.txt` — so every
saved result documents that the correction was recognized in the data
rather than skipped by mistake.

**A batch records the decision as well as the verdict.** The run does not
stop, so the file says what was done in spite of what the headers
claimed. `result_RV_curve.txt` adds, under the verdict line:

```
# NOTE: v_bary was added to every epoch even though 2 header(s) say the
# wavelengths are already barycentric - if those headers are right, the
# correction is counted twice
```

and the GUI batch's `result_batch_report.txt` carries the same thing in
its settings block, on a `wavelength fr.` line next to `barycentric`. The
reverse case is recorded too: with the correction off and topocentric
files in the queue, the line says the epochs keep the Earth's motion.

The report gives a per-file table (instrument, frame keyword, pipeline
v_bary, verdict), the evidence cards for each file, and a closing
recommendation: all corrected → run `batch`/`bf`/`ccf` with `--no-bary`
(v_bary is then computed and written to the result file as a note only,
never added to the RVs); all topocentric → run normally; mixed → split
the runs. `--output report.txt` saves the report.

## 8. BF components: automatic search and your own estimates

Without guesses the components are found automatically by **iterative
fit-and-subtract**: one Gaussian is fitted first, the next component is
seeded on the largest coherent structure left in the residual (smoothed on
the `--min-sep` velocity scale, so single-bin noise spikes are ignored),
and the model is refitted at each step. This finds a faint secondary far
from the primary — whose own shoulder wiggles would win a naive
highest-local-maxima search — and a narrow tertiary superposed on the broad
primary at nearly the same RV.

When the automatic search is not enough, give the fit your own starting
point (the reference-notebook workflow): run once, inspect the smoothed BF
in `result_BF.png`, then rerun with per-component estimates —
`--guess RV1 RV2 ...` (x-axis positions), `--guess-amp A1 A2 ...` (peak
heights on the y-axis) and optionally `--guess-sigma S1 S2 ...` (widths in
km/s). Give one value per component, largest Gaussian first and smallest
last — the order sets the C1/C2/... labels. With amplitudes given,
`curve_fit` starts directly from your `[amp, RV, sigma]` values, exactly
like the reference notebook.

## 9. BF velocity step and the reliability of the RV errors

The Gaussians are fitted to the BF **sampled on a grid of step `dv`**, so a
component narrower than a bin or two is not constrained by the data:
`curve_fit` still returns a centre, but the covariance is nearly singular
and the reported error becomes meaningless — values of 10² to 10⁴ km/s, or
`nan`. Three guards handle this automatically.

**1. The default step follows the instrument.** With `--spectrograph` or
`--resolution` given, `dv` defaults to the instrumental FWHM divided by
4, so four bins always span the narrowest profile the
spectrograph can produce — 1.0 km/s for SOPHIE (R = 75 000), 0.65 km/s for
HARPS. Without a resolution the reference step of 5 km/s is used. The
step is never finer than the median velocity pixel of the data, which is
the real limit: oversampling a correlated profile shrinks the *formal*
errors without improving the velocities.

**2. An undersampled component triggers a refit.** If a fitted sigma spans
fewer than 2 bins, the BF is recomputed on a finer grid and the fit
repeated (at most 2 passes). Two limits
stop it: the median velocity pixel of the data, and — because the BF is
smoothed to the instrumental profile — half the instrumental sigma. A
component as narrow as the instrumental width is *unresolved* and stays so
however fine the grid gets; sampling past that point only adds correlated
bins, which shrink the formal error without adding information. When a
limit is reached the run says so and marks the errors as a lower limit:

```
Unresolved: the component at RV = -6.48 km/s (sigma = 1.67 km/s) —
narrower than 2 x dv = 1.70 km/s, but the profile is already sampled to
the instrumental width (4.00 km/s FWHM), so a finer BF grid would add no
information. The RV error(s) are formal values from correlated bins;
treat them as a lower limit.
```

Otherwise the refinement happens and is reported:

```
Velocity step auto-refined 5.00 -> 2.44 km/s: the component at
RV = 37.14 km/s (sigma = 9.76 km/s) — too narrow for the previous grid
(< 2 bins). The RVs and errors reported are from the refined grid;
pass --dv to fix the step yourself.
```

Passing `--dv` explicitly switches the refinement off — an explicit step is
your decision — but the undersampling is still reported, with the
step that would fix it.

**3. Degenerate multi-component fits are flagged and re-measured.** Near
conjunction the two stars merge into one BF peak; splitting it into two
Gaussians is degenerate (the pair slides along itself at almost constant
χ²), which shows up as a huge or `nan` error. When any component's error
exceeds `BF_DEGENERATE_ERR` (5 km/s), or the components sit closer than the
instrumental FWHM, the same profile is also fitted with a single component
and that result is reported alongside — the one velocity that *is*
measured:

```
Blended / degenerate fit: C2 has an RV error of 25.5 km/s. The
multi-component split is not constrained by the data. Single-component
fit of the same profile: RV = 68.2389 +/- 0.0846 km/s, sigma = 6.34 km/s.
```

In `result_BF.txt` this comes back as a `# blend_single` row; in `batch`
mode all such notes are collected in a `# --- fit-quality notes ---` block
at the top of the RV-curve file. If the single-component fit is not well
constrained either, no number is quoted rather than a misleading one.

Because the per-bin BF amplitude scales with `dv`, `amp` values are only
comparable between runs made with the same step.

### Bayesian velocities and errors

`bf` and `batch` report **Bayesian** velocities and errors. The guards
above still run: the BF is solved, smoothed and fitted first, and that fit
gives the component labels and the starting point.
Then the same profile model is fitted a second time — not to the smoothed
BF, but **to the spectrum itself, through the BF design matrix**:

```
y_t = sum_j D_tj b_j + c0 + c1 x_t + e_t       (one wavelength segment)
b_j = sum_k a_k K(u_j; v_k, w_k) + c_bf
```

`y` are the line depths (1 − flux) on the BF's log-λ grid, `D` holds the
template line depths shifted to every BF velocity `u_j` (Rucinski's linear
BF problem), and `b` is the profile: one Gaussian (or, with
`--profile rot`, one rotational × instrumental profile) per component, with
area `a_k`, velocity `v_k` and width `w_k`. For the unsmoothed BF this is
the BF together with its full noise covariance, so neither the smoothing
nor the SVD cut enters the result. The **posterior** is sampled with
emcee (32 walkers × 8000 steps):

- **RV = posterior median, error = half the 16th–84th percentile range**;
  the report gives both sides (`-0.0125 +0.0127`).
- **Noise.** The spectrum is cut into segments — 5 parts of about 10 nm of
  a merged spectrum (`--bf-segments`), or one per echelle order. Each part
  has its own straight-line continuum, its own noise level and
  **correlated noise**: an AR(p) model whose order is chosen by BIC
  (typically p ≈ 5–15). The residuals of a real spectrum are correlated
  over a line width (template mismatch, resampling), and a white-noise
  model gives errors 2–4 times too small. The continuum (flat prior) and
  the noise level (Jeffreys prior) are integrated out analytically; the AR
  coefficients come from the residuals of the posterior maximum.
- **Priors.** Uniform velocities (labels kept in the order of the profile
  fit), positive areas, widths between half a BF bin and 300 km/s. The
  lower limit is not cosmetic: the template is shifted between the grid
  steps by linear interpolation, and a narrower kernel blends two shifted
  templates by an amount that depends on where the velocity falls between
  the steps. The lines are then sharpest at a grid step, and the
  velocities lock onto the grid. From half a bin on, the blend no longer
  depends on that position. (With templates that already carry the
  stars' line width, as in the benchmarks, the fitted kernel goes to this
  limit; it adds about 1 % to the line width.)
- **Missing data.** Zero-filled detector gaps — the 530.4–533.7 nm gap of
  HARPS ADP s1d products — are left out of the likelihood. The SVD BF
  leaves the same rows out of its design matrix and its data (the run
  prints `BF: N rows on a zero-filled gap ... left out of the SVD`), so
  the profile fit does not see the gap as a 3 nm "line" of depth 1.
  A gap is a run of at least 5 pixels with zero (or negative) flux.

`--bf-error laplace` gives the posterior maximum and its Hessian error in
about a second (the same answer for a well-separated pair); `--bf-error fit`
gives the profile fit of the smoothed BF instead. The report and
`result_BF.txt` show the profile fit beside the Bayesian values:

```
RV error C1: Bayesian (posterior median, 16-84 %) -35.7701 -0.0125 +0.0123 km/s; profile fit of the smoothed BF -35.7640 ± 0.0157
Bayesian BF: 5 segments, AR noise orders 9, 10, 10, 27, 5, 1858 gap pixels left out; emcee 32 x 8000, tau 82
```

and a `batch` curve file gets a `# bayes` row per epoch (errors below and
above the median, the profile-fit RV and error, the AR orders).

**Why.** The profile fit of the smoothed BF fits correlated BF bins as if
they were independent points. Its velocities lose most of the information
in the spectrum, and its errors are not those of the velocities. On 35
synthetic SB2 spectra of known velocity (HARPS-like, S/N 80, 500–550 nm)
the profile fit scattered 75/111 m/s with stated errors of 37/55 m/s,
while the Bayesian velocities scattered 4.9/5.8 m/s with stated errors of
4.4/6.6 m/s (pull rms 1.10/0.87); with a deliberately mismatched template
the Bayesian errors stayed calibrated or conservative (pull rms
0.95/0.63). On the benchmark binaries (the same HARPS and FEROS spectra
as the [benchmark report](README.md#benchmark)) the Bayesian velocities
scatter 14–61 % less about their own orbit — LL Aqr 0.025/0.044 km/s, AK
For 0.096/0.140 km/s — and agree 2–3 times better with the published
velocities of the same spectra.

**What the per-epoch error cannot contain.** It is the measurement error
of one spectrum: the photon noise, through the BF model. A model error —
template mismatch, a profile shape the model does not have, spots, a
blend, an instrument zero point that drifts from night to night (FEROS:
about 0.1 km/s) — is invisible in a single spectrum and does not get
smaller at a higher S/N. The orbit estimates it from the scatter of all
epochs and adds it: total error √(err² + s²), see
[The model error and the total RV error](#the-model-error-and-the-total-rv-error);
for an RV table or figure use those total errors (`result_orbit_rv.txt`).
On the KX Aqr spectra (FEROS and HARPS) the model
error is 0.6–0.7 km/s against measurement errors of 0.45–0.6 km/s, while
two spectra of one night agree within the measurement errors (pull rms
0.8): the measurement error is right, and the model error comes in from
night to night.

Run time of the Bayesian step on a 6-core desktop: about 8 s for the two
narrow components of the BF example (section 2), about 22 s for three
broad ones (KX Aqr: 50 nm of HARPS data at Δv = 0.65 km/s). The BLAS
threads are held at the physical cores during this step, because more
threads only slow its small matrix products.

### Confidence level of the saved errors

Every error RVCalPy computes is **1σ — a 68.27 % confidence interval**:
the CCF width and chunk errors, the TODCOR errors, the Bayesian BF
errors (half the 16th–84th percentile range) and the orbit's posterior
errors. The fits use them so. A Save can also give the interval at
another confidence level — **Confidence** in the Save dialog, chosen after
the analysis, or `--conf LEVEL` on the command line. The interval is
written **beside** the 1σ error, never instead of it, and the fit does not
change. A larger level gives a wider interval of the **same** uncertainty;
the measurement is not less certain. Say the level wherever you quote such
a value ("K₁ = 110.90 ± 0.40 km/s, 99.73 %"); a reader takes a bare ± as
1σ, and an RV figure with 99.73 % error bars has its points about a third
of a bar away from the curve.

How the interval is found:

- **A posterior** (the Bayesian BF velocities, the orbit parameters): its
  percentiles (1 − level)/2 and (1 + level)/2 — when each tail holds at
  least 10 independent samples. A 99.73 % interval needs about 7 400 of
  them; the BF chain (32 × 8000 steps) has about 3 000 and the orbit
  chain (64 × 5000) about 4 500, so at that level the interval is the two
  1σ distances × the normal quantile (3.000 at 99.73 %, 2.968 at 99.7 %),
  and the report says so. Raise `--bf-steps` or `--mcmc-steps` (or
  **Steps** in the GUI's Orbit box) to get the percentiles instead. A
  normal interval of a bounded parameter is cut at its prior edge (rho at
  −1 and 1, s at 0).
- **A chunk error** (CCF and TODCOR, the adopted max(formal, chunks)):
  Student's t with K − 1 degrees of freedom, as for its 1σ value — with 5
  chunks the 99.7 % interval is **5.6 ×** the 1σ error, not 3 ×, because
  five chunks know their own scatter only roughly.
- **Any other error** (the BF profile fit, a formal CCF or TODCOR error,
  the total error of the orbit's RV table, least-squares or Laplace
  errors): the 1σ error × the normal quantile, each side with its own 1σ
  distance.

Factors of the normal quantile: 90 % 1.645, 95 % 1.960, 95.45 % (2σ) 2.000,
99 % 2.576, 99.7 % 2.968, 99.73 % (3σ) 3.000. Example, the BF example of
section 2 at 99.73 %: C1 −35.7701, interval −35.8075 .. −35.7333 km/s
(1σ: −0.0125 +0.0123); at 95 % −35.7942 .. −35.7464 from the posterior
percentiles.

## 10. Component labels

### BF

By default the fitted components are labelled by BF area: **C1 is the
component with the largest light contribution** (profile integral). Two
overrides are available:

- `--guess RV1 RV2 ...` pins the labels to your initial guesses
  (C1 = first guess, C2 = second, ...);
- with an ephemeris (`--t0/--period`, or `--varastro`) the labels follow the
  orbital phase: C1 = primary, the star that is blueshifted just after the
  primary eclipse (with three components the narrowest peak is listed last
  as the tertiary). **T0 must be a primary-eclipse time** — VarAstro's is;
  a T0 from an RV orbit (periastron, maximum velocity) moves the rule.

#### Eccentric orbits: give `--ecc` and `--omega`

The phase rule has to know when the two RV curves cross γ, because that is
when the stars change sides. In a circular orbit they cross at the eclipses,
phases 0 and 0.5, and that is assumed unless you say otherwise. An eccentric
orbit's curves cross somewhere else, and **between that crossing and the
eclipse the circular rule puts the secondary in C1**. On simulated orbits:

| e | phases mislabelled (worst ω) |
|---|---|
| 0.05 | 3 % |
| 0.1 | 6 % |
| 0.3 | 20 % |
| 0.5 | 35 % |

Give the orbit's shape and the crossing is computed instead of assumed:

```bash
python rv_analysis.py batch ... --varastro --ecc 0.3180 --omega 155.48
```

`--ecc` is the eccentricity and `--omega` the argument of periastron **of the
primary** in degrees — the two numbers `orbit --eccentric` prints. So the
usual order is: run the series once, let `orbit` (which finds and repairs
the swapped epochs against the orbit itself) fit e and ω, then run the
series again with them. A
literature orbit works too, as long as its ω is the primary's; some papers
quote the secondary's, 180° away. With `--ecc 0` the rule is the circular
one.

LL Aqr (e = 0.32) is the worked case. Without the shape, the three HARPS
epochs at φ = 0.394, 0.434 and 0.483 come out with C1 and C2 the wrong way
round. Graczyk et al. (2016) have the two that are in their table with the
stars the other way round from the circular rule, and C1's fit error gives
all three away: 0.13–0.22 km/s, the secondary's, against 0.08–0.10 km/s for
the primary at every other epoch. With `--ecc 0.3180 --omega 155.48` — what
`orbit --eccentric` fits to that same series — those three epochs, and only
those, come out right.

The rule that was used is recorded: `result_RV_curve.txt` carries a
`# C1/C2 labels:` line (orbital phase with the orbit shape, BF area, or your
guesses), the GUI batch report a `C1/C2 labels` row, and each BF result its
`Component labels` note.

The light ratios reported with multi-component fits come from the fitted
profile integrals (the `area` values in the result summary; the figure
legend shows each component's `amp` — the real peak height on the BF
axis — and RV).

### TODCOR

Different rule, and an important one. It depends on the templates:

- **Two different templates** (`--template2` given): `rv1`/`rv2` are **the
  template order**, nothing more. Component 1 is whatever velocity was found
  for `--template1`.
- **One template for both** (Template 2 empty — the usual setup) with α
  fitted: the correlation surface is exactly mirror-symmetric, so the run
  fixes the convention itself — **component 1 is the brighter star** (α ≤ 1).
  Near α = 1 the two can still trade places between epochs. A fixed
  `--alpha` is left in the order you asked for.

TODCOR never uses the ephemeris, so with one `bf` and TODCOR can name the stars
differently: `bf` calls C1 the star eclipsed at primary minimum, TODCOR the
brighter one. Usually that is the same star, not always — in a W UMa
W-subtype the deeper eclipse is of the smaller, hotter star. And neither is
sorted by velocity, so they do not line up with a velocity-sorted reference
column such as MIDAS `VA`/`VB` (which has `VA < VB` by construction): a pair
that looks swapped against one is usually the same measurement, differently
labelled — compare the *values*, not the numbering.

Identify TODCOR components by the **`Brighter`** line (label-independent) or by
orbital phase. See
[`todcor` § component labels](#component-labels-are-a-convention-not-a-measurement).

### The Wilson plot settles both questions at once

Neither of the rules above needs an orbit, but neither of them tells you which
star is the more **massive** one. The `wilson` command does, and it repairs
mis-labelled epochs at the same time — see
[`wilson`](#wilson--mass-ratio-from-the-rv1-vs-rv2-plot). Run it once the
series is complete; it is the cheapest consistency check there is on a set of
SB2 velocities.

A line is checked only when the model itself shows it: its minimum lies on the catalogue wavelength, it is at least 2 % deep, and at least half as deep as the deepest feature in its window. The strongest **4 per 50-nm band over 400–600 nm** and 2 per band elsewhere are kept, only inside the analysed range, and never two whose windows overlap (Balmer ±30 Å, He ±10–12 Å, metals ±6 Å). Na I D is excluded, since interstellar absorption is in no stellar model. With more than 4 lines the figure has two columns. **Strong-line fallback**: when the metal lines of the window are poor — fewer than 2, or their median r below 0.5 — the check adds the first two of **Ca II H & K → Mg I b triplet → Hβ → Hα** that can be checked, so **Hα is the last option**. They are looked for in the **whole spectrum**, mostly outside the RV window, so they test the same model where the RV was not measured. A line counts as available only where the data fill its panel, the template covers it and the observed pixel-to-pixel S/N is at least 3 (a noise-only panel judges the noise, not the solution). Observed and model are both normalized locally, by a straight line through each panel's upper envelope, so a raw spectrum and the never-reached continuum around Ca II H & K are compared fairly. These panels are titled "(fallback)" and their rows in `result_*.txt` carry the same name. On Neo-Narval epochs measured in 634–653 nm the result is often Hα alone: Ca II H & K are not in the 5215 K template (it starts at 450 nm), Hβ falls in orders the pipeline could not normalize, and the Mg b orders sit at S/N ≈ 2. **CCF:** the CCF measures no line-broadening kernel (its peak width also contains the template's own lines, and for hot stars it is far from Gaussian), so the Gaussian kernel is fitted to the non-hydrogen windows with a free continuum level and depth scale per window. RV and template stay fixed, and the depth ratios printed are those of the unscaled model. **Reading it:** a good RV with a matching template gives r ≈ 1 and depth O/M ≈ 1. A line at the right place but with the wrong strength (He I deeper and metal lines weaker than the model, say) means the template has the wrong temperature, not the wrong RV |
| `result_TODCOR.txt` / `.png` | `todcor` mode: both velocities with errors, one row per component — with the correction applied as `RV_raw`, `RV_raw_err`, `RV_bary`, `RV_bary_err` and `bary_corr` (the v_bary added), like `result_CCF.txt` / `result_BF.txt`; with `--no-bary` as `RV`, `RV_err` and a `# v_bary` comment line — the fitted α and which component is brighter, the peak correlation, and whether the exact or regular normalization ran. | `result_CCF_profile.txt` / `result_BF_profile.txt` / `result_TODCOR_profile.txt` | The measured profile as plain whitespace-separated columns, for plotting it outside the program — one row per velocity sample. The header line names the columns; plot e.g. column 1 against column 3 (`gnuplot: plot "result_BF_profile.txt" u 1:3`, or `numpy.loadtxt(...)` and columns 0 and 2). | `result_CCF_shifted.fits` / `result_BF_shifted_C<k>.fits` / `result_TODCOR_shifted_C<k>.fits` | The analysed spectrum moved to the star's rest frame, one file per component (no `_C<k>` when there is one). Every order of the input file is shifted by λ_rest = λ / (1 + RV_raw/c). The shift is the **measured** velocity `RV_raw`, the shift actually present in the file's wavelengths; the barycentric correction moves no pixel, so it is not part of the shift. **Primary HDU:** 1-D flux on a uniform linear grid in Å (`CRVAL1`/`CDELT1`/`CRPIX1`, `CTYPE1 = WAVE`, step = the finest input pixels; overlapping orders averaged), for IRAF `splot`, iSpec or any 1-D FITS viewer. **`SPECTRUM` extension:** binary table `WAVE` [Å], `FLUX`, `ORDER` with the shifted pixels exactly, no resampling; `read_spectrum` reads this table, so a re-run uses the exact pixels. ### The run log

Every run — command line, GUI or terminal wizard — appends its complete
console output to **`rv_analysis.log`** in the current directory. An RV is
only as good as the settings it was measured with, so the log is what lets a
number in `result_BF.txt` be traced back to the run that produced it months
later: which spectrum, which template, which step, which version of numpy.

Each run starts with a header block and ends with the status and the elapsed
time:

```
======================================================================
rv_analysis.py run started 2026-08-10 22:03:11
======================================================================
command    : rv_analysis.py bf --spectrum examples/example_BF_HARPS_norm.txt --template examples/template_*.txt --spectrograph HARPS --components 2 --dv 1 --no-bary
directory  : /home/user/work/*
python     : 3.12.3 (/home/user/venv/bin/python)
packages   : numpy 2.5.1, scipy 1.18.0, astropy 8.0.1, matplotlib 3.11.0, PyAstronomy 0.25.0, emcee 3.1.6, corner 2.3.0
platform   : Linux-7.0.0-28-generic-x86_64-with-glibc2.39
======================================================================

... the full output of the run ...

======================================================================
run finished in 29.9 s (2026-08-10 22:03:41)
======================================================================
```

Everything the run prints is captured: the results, the sampling-guard
notices ([section 9](#9-bf-velocity-step-and-the-reliability-of-the-rv-errors)),
the barycentric verdicts, every warning, and — when a run stops on a bad
input or crashes — the error message and the full traceback. The closing
line then reads `run stopped with an error` or `run failed` instead of
`run finished`.

- Runs are **appended**, newest last, so one file keeps the history of a
  whole reduction session. Delete it whenever you want a fresh start.
- `--log FILE` puts the log somewhere else (`--log logs/*.log`; missing
  directories are created), which is the convenient way to keep one log per
  target.
- `--no-log` switches it off.
- The log never interferes with the analysis: if the file cannot be written,
  the run warns once and carries on.

## 12. Troubleshooting

| Symptom | What it means / what to do |
|---|---|
| *"this is a RAW … frame … an unextracted CCD exposure, not a spectrum"* | You downloaded a CCD exposure (`HARPS.*`/`FEROS.*.fits`). Run `fetch-adp` to get the reduced `ADP.*.fits` and analyze those |
| `WARNING: the header of this spectrum says its wavelength scale is already barycentric` | The run continues and v_bary is added. Decide from the evidence: `barycheck` prints it card by card, and a **BJD** among those cards proves nothing about the wavelengths. If the header is right, re-run with `--no-bary`; `--force-bary` only silences the warning — see [section 7](#7-barycentric-frame-what-the-headers-say-and-who-decides) |
| RV error of 10²–10⁴ km/s, or `nan` | The component is undersampled or the multi-component split is degenerate. See [section 9](#9-bf-velocity-step-and-the-reliability-of-the-rv-errors); pass `--dv`, or trust the reported single-component fit |
| Two BF peaks merge into one near conjunction | Physical, not a bug: at conjunction the components have the same RV. Use the `# blend_single` value, or drop that epoch |
| A component sits at the edge of the BF window | Widen `--vel-range` |
| BF finds only one component, or rails one at ±`--vel-range` | Check the separation against the profile width first: two components closer than about one FWHM have no saddle to fit and the split is not constrained by the data, whatever seeds you give it. Switch to `todcor` |
| TODCOR `rv1`/`rv2` look swapped against MIDAS `VA`/`VB` | They almost certainly are not — `VA`/`VB` is velocity-sorted, `rv1`/`rv2` is template order (brightness order with one template). Compare the values and use the `Brighter` line ([section 10](#todcor)) |
| TODCOR labels flip between epochs or wavelength ranges | The mirror maximum. Expected with one template (the two maxima are exactly equal); with two *near-identical* templates it means they are too similar to separate — use one template for both, or genuinely different ones |
| The TODCOR separation sits right at `--min-sep` | The constraint is setting it, not the data (the peak lands on the first allowed cell, one pixel outside the band). Lower or drop `--min-sep` |
| The fitted α looks implausible | Check how flat the maximum is before treating it as a measurement ([Reading α](#reading-α-the-light-ratio)); fix it with `--alpha` if you know it independently |
| The BF is noisy / wings do not decay | Check that both spectrum **and** template are continuum-normalized; give `--spectrograph`/`--resolution` so the BF is smoothed properly; try `--svd-rcond` |
| The CCF peak is at a nonsense velocity | Low S/N is the dominant source of spurious RVs. Check the S/N in the result file; restrict the range with `--wave-min/--wave-max`; for a hot star keep the default mask mode |
| Hot star gives a broad, biased CCF | Do not pass `--keep-balmer`; the default mask already drops the hydrogen series |
| Ten RAW spectra to measure | Queue them in **Batch...** and tick **Normalize the queued spectra first**. It asks for a directory for the normalized copies, fits a cubic B-spline continuum to each, then measures those. There is nothing to configure; use **Normalize raw...** if you want to tune one spectrum by eye first |
| A batch measured the blaze instead of the star | The spectra were raw. Every method here needs a continuum at 1.0 — re-run with the batch normalization ticked, or normalize them beforehand |
| Ten spectra to measure with one template | Set the window up once, press **Batch...**, queue the files, and read every fit in the results overview — see [Batch](#batch-a-whole-series-in-one-run). For an unattended run over a long series use the command-line `batch` instead |
| A batch asks: "the queue mixes two wavelength frames" | Some queued files say their wavelengths are already barycentric and others say they are topocentric. The **Apply barycentric correction** tick is one switch, so it cannot suit both groups unless one claim is wrong — answer No and run them as two batches, or Yes if you know better than the headers; `barycheck` shows the per-file evidence |
| A batch refuses to start: "a batch needs the automatic component search" | BF assumption mode starts every fit from RVs read off one epoch's curve, which drags a whole series towards that epoch's answer. Open **Method...** and switch to the automatic search |
| Batch results all share one BJD or phase | Not possible from the GUI batch, which ignores the **Obs. time** box and reads each file's own header. If it happens on the command line, `--obstime` was given one value for the whole series — drop it, or use `--bjd` with one value per file |
| The GUI does not open | `tkinter` is missing (see [Setup](README.md#setup)) or there is no display — the terminal wizard starts instead with the same steps |
| A run misbehaved and the terminal is gone | Read `rv_analysis.log` — it holds the whole output of every run, including the command line that produced it ([The run log](#the-run-log)) |
| No template file at hand | Give `--teff/--logg/--feh` to download a PHOENIX model (needs `expecto`) |
| VarAstro finds nothing | The lookup already retries the standard spelling (`kx-aqr` → `KX Aqr`), the zero-padded GCVS spelling and the SIMBAD cross-identifiers; if it still fails, pass `--t0/--period` yourself |
| SIMBAD does not find the star | Hyphens, underscores, lower case, a missing space and an ESO programme number (`2234_LL-Aqr`) are already handled; a name that still fails is not a SIMBAD identifier — try a catalogue name (HD, TYC, Gaia) or give `--ra/--dec` |
| The GUI shows the previous spectrum's target | The fields the program filled in are cleared when the spectrum path changes, and `Header` refills them from the file now loaded. Fields you typed yourself are kept and reported rather than overwritten — clear them by hand if they belonged to the previous target |
| A file with no header gives OBJECT/DATE-OBS = None | If its name follows a convention RVCalPy knows (DAO, Demetra — see [metadata in the file name](#metadata-in-the-file-name)) the values are read from the name; `header --resolve` shows what was found. Otherwise pass `--object/--obstime/--site` yourself |
| A Demetra spectrum picks the wrong observatory | That convention does not encode the site and the run falls back to the default, saying so. Pass `--site` (`kreiken` for the AUKR T80/Whoppshel spectra); the raw `.fit` frames carry it in `GEO_LONG`/`GEO_LAT`/`GEO_ELEV` and are read automatically |
| The BJDs of a series all sit near the start of their day (`.50…`), the phases are scattered and v_bary differs from the header's by tenths of km/s | The files' `DATE-OBS` holds only a date. The time then comes from `MJD-OBS` (or `TM-START`); a normalized copy that holds only the date, and whose original has moved, says so with a WARNING. Re-normalize from the original, or give `--bjd` — see [Observation time](#observation-time) |
| A batch reports `v_bary = +0.0000` and no BJD ("barycentric correction skipped (needs coordinates AND observation time)") | The queued files carry no epoch. If they are copies made by `normalize`, the source file named in their comment header is read, and with it any PolarBase `.meta` near *it* ([section 3](#metadata-in-a-sidecar-file-polarbase)). If they are cuts made by hand with no header at all, give `--obstime` (one file) or `--bjd` (one value per file), or re-cut them from the original product |
| RVs offset by 20-25 km/s from a published table | A spectrum whose header does not record the wavelength frame may already be barycentric; the header check cannot see it and the run says so. Compare one epoch both ways and use `--no-bary` if the uncorrected value is the one that matches ([section 7](#7-barycentric-frame-what-the-headers-say-and-who-decides)) |
| A PolarBase spectrum has no epoch or coordinates | A `.s` (or a window cut out of it) finds its `.meta` only by the same file name, or as the single `.meta` in its folder ([sidecar](#metadata-in-a-sidecar-file-polarbase)). A copy in a folder with no `.meta` (or several) has none. Normalize it in its own folder first, or use the `.fts`, which has its own header |
| A PolarBase file warns: header says already barycentric | Expected, and the run continues. PolarBase's own documentation says its wavelengths are heliocentric, and its `SPECSYS = HELIOCENTER` says so too; the date cards beside it say nothing either way and are not read. With `--no-bary` (box unticked) the helio→barycentric term, at most ~13 m/s, is added instead and written in the `helio_to_bary[km/s]` column; with the box ticked the full v_bary is added and the run warns |
| `batch` warns that the spectra mix the two PolarBase products | Some files are the pipeline-normalized `<odo>i`, others the `<odo>i_u`. Their zero points differ by ~0.15 km/s. Queue one product only, preferably `i` |
| The same PolarBase exposure gives very different RVs as `i` and `i_u` | The `i_u` flux is not normalized (it still carries the blaze). Normalize it first, or measure the `i` product |
| The line check shows lines far deeper or shallower than the model | Look at *which* lines. Lines in the right place with the wrong strength (He I deeper and metals weaker in a B star, say) mean the template's temperature is wrong, not the RV. A model with no line where the data have one never appears, because only lines the model shows are checked |
| Two Wilson runs of the same table give a different slope, rms and robust σ | Compare three things in the two `<star>_mass_ratio.txt` files. **The error columns**: the lines `Error on V1/V2: column …` at the top, and the per-epoch rows — `nan` there means the fit ran unweighted. **The swap repair**: on by default from the command line, off by default in the GUI dialog (`--no-fix-swaps` matches the GUI); a `SWAPPED` row means it changed the data. **Epochs left out** by a click in the GUI: the `Excluded by hand` line under `Epochs used`. Same columns, same swap setting and same epochs give the same numbers to the last digit ([how they are computed](#wilson--mass-ratio-from-the-rv1-vs-rv2-plot)) |
| The Wilson legend's slope does not match the M₂/M₁ in the title | It is not supposed to: the figure has V₁ across, so its line has slope −M₁/M₂ = −1/q, the reciprocal of the title's q (the Swap axes button draws it the other way, slope −q). The two look alike only for a near-twin, q ≈ 1. The report's `Slope` is −q |
| The orbital phase looks wrong | Check the two dates against each other. Both `--t0` and `--obstime` may be full or reduced by 2 400 000 and are promoted to full JD with a printed note ([Julian dates](#julian-dates-full-or-reduced)) — but an **MJD** is silently taken as a reduced JD and lands half a day off; give an MJD as `MJD + 2400000.5` |

of options.
